# Listen & Learn — Chapter 8: The Promised al-Mahdi, Part 2

## Narration
This final chapter brings together history, belief and practical responsibility through the subject of the promised al-Mahdi. Mutahhari does not begin by treating Mahdism as an idea that suddenly appeared around the birth of Imam al-Mahdi in the third century after the Hijrah. His historical argument is that expectations of a promised reformer and a future triumph of justice were already present in earlier Islamic thought and practice. The chapter therefore asks how this belief developed, how it was used, and what it means for a believer's conduct.

Mutahhari points to Qur'anic promises about righteous servants inheriting the earth and the eventual establishment of true religion as broad foundations for hope in a future transformation. He also emphasizes that reports about the Mahdi are found in Sunni as well as Shi'ah collections. His purpose is not simply to count references; he wants to show that the expectation had a wide place in Muslim religious consciousness. At the same time, the existence of a report, the reliability of a particular narration and the interpretation drawn from it are distinct questions. They should not be collapsed into one claim.

A central idea in the chapter is the report attributed to Imam 'Ali in his conversation with Kumayl: the earth is never empty of God's proof, whether that proof is publicly manifest or living in a less visible condition. Mutahhari uses this idea to connect belief in the Imam with a broader account of divine guidance through history. This is a theological claim within the tradition the book is presenting, and the narration should make that status clear rather than presenting it as a conclusion reached by historical method alone.

The chapter then turns to early movements that used Mahdist expectations. Mukhtar's movement associated Muhammad ibn Hanafiyyah with the promised Mahdi. After Muhammad ibn Hanafiyyah's death, some followers developed the belief that he had not died but was hidden at Mount Radwa. Mutahhari presents this as an early example of how a belief can shape political and religious movements, while also showing that claims about a particular person's identity may be disputed.

The discussion of Zayd ibn 'Ali and the statement attributed to the scholar Zuhri is used to argue that expectations of a future successful rising from the Prophet's family were recognizable before the birth of Imam al-Mahdi. The case of Muhammad ibn 'Abd Allah, known as Nafs al-Zakiyyah, offers another example. Because of his lineage, name and the political hopes surrounding him, some people connected his uprising with Mahdism. Imam al-Sadiq, according to the reports discussed, refused to endorse the claim that Muhammad was the promised Mahdi, while distinguishing that question from whether a movement against wrongdoing could have a legitimate moral purpose.

This distinction is crucial. Supporting a call to enjoin good and forbid evil is not the same as accepting a claimant's identity as the promised Mahdi. A movement can invoke a religious ideal without proving that its leader fulfills that ideal. Mutahhari points to repeated Mahdist claims in history as evidence that the idea had influence, but the recurrence of a claim does not by itself prove the truth of each claimant's assertion.

The Abbasid use of the name al-Mahdi further illustrates the political importance of the concept. The chapter describes how rulers could adopt the title or invoke the expectation for purposes of legitimacy. The case of Muhammad ibn 'Ijlan, who pledged allegiance to a claimant, shows the danger of confusing a popular movement or a person's title with proof of identity. The historical record must be examined carefully: belief in the promised Mahdi, political appropriation of that belief and the identity of any specific claimant are related but separate matters.

Mutahhari argues that early disagreements often concerned which person was the Mahdi rather than whether there would be a promised Mahdi at all. The account of Imam al-Sadiq publicly demonstrating the death of his son Isma'il is used to show how misunderstandings about succession and identity could generate false expectations. The lesson is methodological: a person's name, lineage, reputation or death cannot be ignored when evaluating a claim, and evidence must be examined rather than replaced by wishful thinking.

The chapter also refers to Di'bil's lamentation before Imam al-Rida, in which the tragedies of the Ahl al-Bayt are remembered and the hope of a future figure who would rise is invoked. Mutahhari uses such material to connect Mahdism to the memory of oppression and the expectation of justice. He also argues that the belief is not exclusively Shi'ah by pointing to Sunni traditions and historical expressions. Reports of false Mahdis are another part of this history: the existence of false claimants does not erase the belief itself, but it does show why discernment is necessary.

References to Mahdist imagery in poetry attributed to Hafiz are treated cautiously. Mutahhari acknowledges uncertainty about whether the poet himself held a specific doctrinal view. This is another example of the book's distinction between a text, a later interpretation and a claim about an author's private belief. A poem may use imagery that resembles a doctrine without proving the poet's complete position on that doctrine.

The chapter then moves from historical survey to the practical meaning of awaiting the Imam. Mutahhari rejects the idea that believers should allow corruption to grow because justice is promised after oppression. The fact that justice will ultimately overcome tyranny is not a command to tolerate, assist or produce tyranny in the present. Waiting for reform does not excuse a person from moral responsibility.

The analogy of an abscess is used to describe a theory in which corruption must accumulate until it breaks open. Mutahhari challenges the careless conclusion that believers should therefore help make society more corrupt. Even if a historical transformation follows a period of injustice, it does not follow that injustice is a religious duty or that reformers should abandon their work. The promised future cannot be used to justify present wrongdoing.

The same principle applies to religious obligations. Awaiting al-Mahdi does not suspend prayer, fasting, zakat, Hajj, enjoining good, forbidding evil or responsibility toward other people. Genuine expectation must coexist with action. A person cannot claim to await justice while neglecting the duties that make his own conduct more just. Waiting is not passive pessimism; in the chapter's conclusion, it is a form of faith expressed through worship, self-discipline, social responsibility and preparation.

Mutahhari's overall evidence chain moves from Qur'anic promises to prophetic reports, statements attributed to Imam 'Ali, early movements and claims, and material from both Sunni and Shi'ah historical traditions. He then draws a philosophical and practical conclusion about justice and responsibility. A careful listener should preserve each step in that chain and distinguish the sources themselves from the author's interpretation of their meaning.

## Deep Reading insight
The chapter asks us to keep three distinctions clear. First, a religious belief is not the same as every political movement that invokes it. Second, historical evidence for a claim is not the same as proof of every interpretation attached to it. Third, hope for future justice is not permission to neglect justice now.

These distinctions are useful well beyond this topic. Whenever a powerful ideal is used to justify a political movement, ask what the ideal actually teaches, what evidence supports the claim being made, and whether the proposed action is consistent with the ideal itself.

## Reflection
What does active waiting look like in daily life? Choose one personal duty and one social responsibility that you should not postpone while hoping for a better future. How could your conduct become a small preparation for the justice you believe in?

## Transition
The book's historical discussion ends here, but its practical question remains open: what kind of person and community are we helping to create while we await justice? The answer begins with today's choices, not with abandoning responsibility to tomorrow.

## Bringing the thread together

The awaited Mahdi is presented as more than a distant prediction. The idea of a promised future can orient the present toward justice, patience, responsibility, and resistance to despair. A careful reading distinguishes hope that encourages action from passivity that postpones moral duty.

As the chapter closes, connect the historical setting, the theological claim, and the ethical consequence. The argument is not complete until we can explain how those layers support one another and what they ask of a thoughtful reader today.
