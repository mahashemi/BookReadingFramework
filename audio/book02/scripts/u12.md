# Listen & Learn — Chapter 8, Part 2

## Narration
The book now brings its historical and philosophical threads together through the idea of the Promised al-Mahdi.

Mutahhari begins by rejecting the idea that Mahdism appeared only when the later Shi'ah doctrine of the hidden Imam took its developed form. He traces the expectation much further back—to Qur'anic promises, Prophetic reports and statements attributed to Imam 'Ali.

But his main historical question is not simply, "Can we find reports about the Mahdi?" It is: what effect did belief in the Mahdi have on Islamic history?

That question opens a fascinating—and dangerous—history.

Different political movements and individuals repeatedly connected themselves with Mahdist expectation. Mukhtar associated Muhammad ibn Hanafiyyah with the promised Mahdi. Later, Muhammad Nafs al-Zakiyyah was connected to the idea because of his lineage and other reported signs. Imam al-Sadiq, however, drew a careful distinction: supporting a movement against oppression was one question; accepting the claim that a particular person was the Promised Mahdi was another.

That distinction is crucial.

The existence of false or mistaken claimants does not by itself disprove the belief they were exploiting. At the same time, the existence of a popular belief does not prove that every person claiming its title is genuine.

Mutahhari repeatedly asks us to separate **the claim** from **the evidence for the claim**.

The historical discussion also shows that Mahdist expectation was not confined to one community. Mutahhari points to Sunni material, political figures, scholars and literary culture to argue that the idea had broad historical resonance.

Then comes one of the most important practical questions: if the world is expected to reach a final age of justice, should believers simply wait for it?

Mutahhari's answer is emphatic: no.

Waiting for the Imam does not cancel prayer, fasting, charity, pilgrimage, enjoining good, forbidding evil or moral responsibility. And believers must not deliberately create corruption on the theory that greater corruption will somehow accelerate reappearance.

The promised future is a reason for hope and responsibility, not an excuse for passivity.

The chapter therefore closes with a powerful chain of ideas: Qur'anic promises, Prophetic reports, the teachings attributed to the Imams, the historical development of Mahdist expectation, the misuse of that expectation by political actors, and finally the practical meaning of awaiting justice.

## Deep Reading insight
Hope becomes spiritually powerful only when it changes what we do now.

If I believe justice is ultimately possible, that belief should make me more—not less—committed to justice in the small decisions available to me today.

## Reflection
Ask yourself: what would "waiting for justice" look like if it meant becoming the kind of person who is already practicing justice?

## Transition
This brings the book to its final synthesis: the different lives and strategies of the Imams are not disconnected stories. They are different historical expressions of a deeper struggle to preserve truth, justice, spiritual formation and responsibility.
