# Listen & Learn — Chapter 8, Part 1

## Narration
The final movement of the book begins with a very large idea: justice is not a side topic of religion. Mutahhari presents two central prophetic missions—establishing the right relationship between human beings and God, and establishing just relationships among human beings.

Universal justice therefore means more than the hope that people will be rewarded after death. It points toward a transformed human society in which tyranny, exploitation, discrimination, hatred and oppression no longer define ordinary life.

Mutahhari organizes the discussion around three questions. What is justice? Is the desire for justice actually present in human nature? And if justice is possible, how can it become real?

His definition is important. Justice does not simply mean giving everyone exactly the same thing. It means giving people what they are entitled to according to their real qualifications, responsibilities and circumstances. Equality and justice can overlap, but they are not identical.

The deeper question is whether a human being can love justice even when injustice would personally benefit him.

Mutahhari examines several answers. One view reduces morality to power. Another makes justice a form of enlightened self-interest: we accept rules because, in the long run, everyone benefits. But Mutahhari points out the weakness of relying on self-interest alone. What happens when someone becomes powerful enough to ignore the consequences?

His Islamic answer is more demanding. Human beings can be educated and spiritually developed until justice becomes something they genuinely desire—even when personal advantage points in another direction.

He points to Imam 'Ali as an example of justice that cannot be explained merely as calculated self-interest.

The discussion then reaches Mahdism. The promised future is not presented as humanity becoming primitive. It is a vision of intellectual, moral and social elevation: greater justice, security, spiritual wealth and a renewed vitality of religious life.

## Deep Reading insight
This gives us a useful distinction between **external control** and **internal character**. A society can restrain injustice through laws, but the deeper achievement is a human being who does not need fear of punishment to choose justice.

## Reflection
Ask yourself a difficult question: would you still defend a principle of fairness if breaking it would benefit you and nobody would ever know?

## Transition
