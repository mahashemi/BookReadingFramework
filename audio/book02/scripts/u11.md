# Listen & Learn — Chapter 8: Universal Justice, Part 1

## Narration
Mutahhari begins the final movement of the book by identifying two essential purposes of prophetic missions. The first is to establish the right relationship between human beings and God through tawhid. The second is to establish just relationships among human beings. These purposes are connected: belief in the one God should not remain an abstract statement while social life is governed by oppression, exploitation and discrimination.

The Qur'anic language about the Book and the Balance is used to support the idea that justice is a general prophetic mission. Mutahhari understands justice as more than a personal virtue or a promise about the afterlife. It points toward a transformation of human society in which tyranny, war, hatred, exploitation and deception no longer dominate. This expectation includes the world in which people live together, not only the world to come.

The chapter organizes the discussion around three questions. What is justice? Is the desire for justice rooted in human nature? And can justice actually be established, or is it only an ideal that people praise while continuing to act from self-interest? These questions move from definition to psychology and then to the practical possibility of social change.

Mutahhari defines justice in terms of giving each person what they are entitled to, in relation to their circumstances, qualifications and actions. Justice is therefore contrasted with oppression and discrimination. But justice does not necessarily mean treating every person identically in every situation. Equal dignity and fair treatment do not always require identical outcomes. For example, a role may require particular competence, or a responsibility may depend on what a person has actually done. The challenge is to distinguish a relevant difference from a pretext used to justify privilege.

The question of human nature follows. Can people value justice for its own sake, even when acting justly brings them no immediate personal benefit? Mutahhari compares the possibility of loving justice with other human capacities, such as appreciating beauty or seeking knowledge. His argument is that human beings are not reducible to appetite and self-interest; they can be educated and morally developed until justice itself becomes something they desire.

The chapter presents several competing views. One position, associated in Mutahhari's account with Nietzsche and Machiavelli, treats power as fundamental and justice as a concept developed by the weak to restrain the strong. On this view, what is called justice may be a strategy of those who lack power rather than a moral truth that binds everyone. Mutahhari presents this view as a serious challenge because it asks whether justice has any force when the powerful can ignore it without consequence.

A second position, associated with Bertrand Russell in the chapter, accepts justice through enlightened self-interest. People eventually recognize that their own welfare depends on a society in which others also receive fair treatment. This view gives a practical reason to support justice: stable cooperation benefits everyone over time. But Mutahhari asks whether calculated interest is enough. What happens when an individual or group becomes powerful enough to exploit others without fear of retaliation? If justice rests only on self-interest, it may disappear precisely when injustice becomes profitable.

The chapter also discusses a Marxist account in which economic conditions and productive relations are expected to change in ways that ultimately produce a more just society. Mutahhari presents this as a theory of historical development. His concern is whether changes in economic structure alone can guarantee moral justice, or whether human beings also need ethical and spiritual formation. Institutions matter, but a society's arrangements do not automatically remove selfishness, domination or the desire to exploit.

Against these positions, Mutahhari presents the Islamic view as more hopeful about the possibility of educating human nature. Human beings can learn to desire justice even when it conflicts with immediate advantage. The point is not that every person is already just or that institutions are unnecessary. It is that moral formation can change what people want and how they act. Justice need not remain a thin agreement among people who are afraid of punishment; it can become a principle that a person chooses because it is right.

Imam 'Ali is offered as an example of commitment to justice that cannot easily be reduced to personal advantage. Mutahhari invokes his refusal to trade justice for political convenience as evidence that a human being can place fairness above self-interest. Such examples do not mean that one person can solve every social problem. They show that the human capacity for justice is not merely theoretical; it can appear in real choices, including choices that carry a cost.

The discussion then turns to Mahdism and the promised future of humanity. Mutahhari rejects the idea that the reappearance of the Imam of the Time would return society to a primitive state. He presents it instead as a stage of intellectual, moral and scientific elevation. Universal justice is not imagined as the destruction of human development, but as a transformation in which knowledge and social life can flourish without being governed by oppression.

The question of the Imam's long life is addressed through an argument about the limits of current knowledge. Mutahhari argues that what science has not yet established should not automatically be treated as impossible in principle. This is his reasoning in the chapter; the reader should distinguish the philosophical argument from a claim that contemporary science has independently proved the doctrine. The broader point is that the limits of what is presently known should be assessed carefully rather than turned into certainty about what can never occur.

Traditions about the age of the Mahdi describe a society marked by justice, security, spiritual richness, reduced poverty and hatred, fair distribution and renewed attention to the Qur'an and Sunnah. Mutahhari links these expectations to the idea that justice can transform both institutions and human relationships. The title al-Qa'im, meaning the one who rises, is connected to the establishment of justice and the restoration of religious guidance.

## Deep Reading insight
The chapter challenges the idea that justice is only a bargain among self-interested people. Laws and institutions are necessary, but they cannot by themselves guarantee justice if people are eager to exploit every opportunity. A just society also requires moral formation: people must learn to recognize others' rights even when injustice would benefit them.

At the same time, moral education should not be used as an excuse to ignore institutional problems. Justice requires both people who value fairness and arrangements that restrain abuse of power. The chapter's question is how these two dimensions can reinforce one another.

## Reflection
Think of a situation in which fairness costs you something: time, money, status or an advantage over someone else. What would motivate you to act justly if no one were watching and no penalty would follow? How might institutions support that choice without replacing moral responsibility?
