# Listen & Learn — Chapter 2: Imam al-Hassan's Pacifism, Session 1

## Narration
Mutahhari opens with a question that can seem troubling at first: why did Imam al-Hassan make peace with Mu'awiyah while Imam al-Husayn later refused to submit to Yazid and rose against him? The author rejects the idea that one Imam was naturally peaceful while another was naturally rebellious. If both are understood as guided leaders, their different actions must be examined in relation to the circumstances, responsibilities and choices available to them.

The Prophet's own life provides the first comparison. During the Meccan period, Muslims were tortured and persecuted, yet they were not permitted to answer every act of violence with armed retaliation. After the migration to Medina, the circumstances changed and permission to fight was given. The Prophet later fought in some situations and accepted agreements in others, including the treaty of Hudaybiyyah. Mutahhari uses this pattern to show that peace and armed struggle are not fixed identities. They are responses whose moral status depends on the situation and on what they can accomplish.

The same pattern appears in the life of Imam 'Ali. After the Prophet's death, 'Ali did not immediately fight to claim the caliphate. He tried to prevent 'Uthman's killing and later accepted arbitration at Siffin under pressure from elements of his own army. These episodes do not mean that 'Ali lacked conviction. Mutahhari argues that a principled leader must distinguish between the justice of a claim and the means available to pursue it. A person may refuse to recognize an unjust authority while still deciding that a particular battle or confrontation would produce a worse outcome.

The chapter then introduces categories of jihad in Shi'ah jurisprudence, drawing on jurists such as Muhaqqiq al-Hilli and Shahid Thani. Different forms of fighting have different conditions. Offensive jihad is not simply a private decision to wage war; the jurisprudential account discussed by Mutahhari places conditions on who may authorize it. Defensive fighting is a different category, and fighting rebellion within a Muslim community raises another set of questions. Even defending one's home or life may belong to a different moral category from a campaign conducted by a political authority.

These distinctions matter because the word jihad can be used as if it described one simple activity with one universal rule. Mutahhari's discussion instead insists on classification. Before judging an action, one must ask what kind of conflict it is, who has authority, what threat is present, and what conditions apply. Without these distinctions, people can take a rule meant for one situation and use it to justify an action in a very different one.

The chapter considers fighting rebellion as a separate case. When two Muslim groups clash, the Qur'anic instruction cited by Mutahhari calls first for reconciliation. Fighting is directed toward the party that refuses a just settlement and continues aggression. This sequence is important: the goal is not to treat disagreement as sufficient reason for violence. Reconciliation, evidence and the actual conduct of the parties must be considered before force is used.

Mutahhari next turns to the jurisprudence of a truce. A peace agreement may be permissible when it serves the community's legitimate interests—for example, when the community lacks the strength to win, needs time to recover, or can prevent greater harm through an agreement. The point is not that peace is always wise or that every treaty is just. It is that refusing peace in every circumstance is not itself proof of courage. The terms, the balance of power and the likely consequences must be examined.

The treaty of Hudaybiyyah is the central precedent. The Prophet approached Mecca as a pilgrim without setting out to fight, but the Quraysh blocked his way. He accepted a truce whose terms could appear one-sided to some of his companions. The agreement included conditions that were difficult to accept emotionally, yet Mutahhari emphasizes the larger outcome: the truce created space for the message of Islam to spread and changed the political situation. What looked like a concession in the immediate moment could serve a greater purpose over time.

The comparison with Imam al-Hassan is therefore not a claim that every peace treaty is identical to Hudaybiyyah. It establishes a principle: peace can be a deliberate strategy when it preserves a community, prevents a worse outcome or creates room for a longer mission. The question is whether the agreement is made under sound judgment and whether its terms serve a legitimate purpose.

In the discussion that follows, a listener asks whether it is circular to judge al-Hassan's conduct by jurisprudence that is itself built from the Imams' precedents. Mutahhari's response is to examine the coherence of the jurisprudential framework as a whole, rather than assuming that one action is justified simply because it resembles a previous action. He also addresses differences between Shi'ah and Sunni jurisprudential discussions of jihad. The methodological lesson is that religious reasoning should be examined carefully: what principle is being applied, what evidence supports it, and how does it fit with the broader tradition?

## Deep Reading insight
This session teaches contextual moral reasoning. A principle cannot always be applied by copying the outward form of a past action. We must identify the relevant similarities and differences. Peace can be cowardice in one context, prudent protection in another, and a way to preserve a mission in a third. Likewise, fighting can be defense, legitimate resistance or an avoidable escalation. The moral label depends on the evidence and circumstances.

## Reflection
Think of a difficult decision where people describe one option as brave and another as weak. What facts would you need before accepting those labels? Consider the available choices, the risks to others, the purpose of the action and its likely long-term consequences.

## A closer walk through the source

The first narration has introduced the chapter's main line of thought. This second pass is deliberately slower: it follows each canonical concept in sequence, retains the source's qualifications, and explains how each point contributes to the argument. The aim is not to repeat a short summary, but to make the reasoning easier to hear and remember.

### Chapter 2 Session 1 — Framing the question of al-Hassan's peace vs al-Husayn's revolt

Mutahhari opens Session 1 by posing the puzzle directly: why did Imam al-Hassan make peace with Mu'awiyah while Imam al-Husayn refused to submit to Yazid? He rejects the easy explanation that the two Imams simply had different temperaments (one peace-loving, one rebellious), arguing instead that a proper answer requires first laying out Islam's general jurisprudential rules on jihad and peace, since both men's choices were reasoned applications of those same rules to different circumstances, not personality traits.

This point also guards against a familiar mistake: lifting one phrase out of its setting and turning it into a universal slogan. Read it beside the surrounding concepts, and notice how the author's reasoning qualifies the conclusion.

### Chapter 2 Session 1 — The Prophet's own alternation between peace and war

Mutahhari shows that pacifism was not unique to al-Hassan: the Prophet himself avoided armed retaliation throughout the Meccan period even as Muslims were tortured, and permission to fight was only revealed after the migration to Medina (citing Qur'an 22:39). The Prophet later both fought polytheists, Jews and Christians at times and signed treaties with them at other times (e.g., the Hudaybiyyah treaty and a non-aggression pact with the Jews of Medina), showing that Islam treats war and peace as circumstance-dependent tools rather than fixed identities.

This concept belongs to the chapter's larger account of Imam Hasan's pacifism and the jurisprudence of peace. It should not be heard as an isolated anecdote: it helps establish the circumstances in which the next moral judgment becomes intelligible. Ask what evidence is being presented, which principle the author applies to it, and what conclusion follows.

### Chapter 2 Session 1 — 'Ali's own record of restraint and arbitration

Mutahhari traces the same pattern in 'Ali: he refused to fight for the caliphate after the Prophet's death (drawing even Fatimah's objection), mediated between the rebels and 'Uthman to try to prevent his murder, and later accepted arbitration at Siffin after Mu'awiyah's side raised the Qur'an on spearheads — even though 'Amr ibn al-'As then manipulated the arbitration to a fruitless outcome. Mutahhari uses this to argue that 'Ali, like the Prophet, moved between war and peace based on the situation, which sets up the same question for al-Hassan and al-Husayn.

### Chapter 2 Session 1 — The categories of jihad in Shi'ah jurisprudence

Mutahhari outlines, citing the jurists Muhaqqiq al-Hilli and Shahid Thani, several distinct categories of jihad: (1) 'antecedent' offensive jihad against non-Muslims, which requires the presence of an imam or his appointee and is not permitted even to a religious authority acting alone; (2) defensive jihad when Islamic land, people, or property are attacked, which becomes obligatory on all Muslims regardless of an imam's permission, with the obligation growing stronger the closer one is to the danger; and (3) a jihad-like category (not classed as general jihad but earning the same martyrdom status) covering a Muslim in non-Muslim lands who must fight to save his own life, or someone defending his home from an attacker, where the level of resistance required scales with what is at stake (property vs. life).

The important question is not only what happened, but what options were available and what each option would have meant for the people involved. The source invites us to weigh intention, practical consequence, and principle together rather than treating any one of them as sufficient by itself.

### Chapter 2 Session 1 — Fighting rebels against a just ruler

A further category is fighting rebels: when two Muslim factions clash, the Qur'an (49:9) commands the community first to seek reconciliation, and only to fight the side that refuses to comply. Mutahhari notes this applies especially when a group rises against a just imam, in which case Muslims are obligated to fight in the imam's favor, and mentions a related but separately debated category of righteous armed uprising for 'enjoining good and forbidding evil.'

This point also guards against a familiar mistake: lifting one phrase out of its setting and turning it into a universal slogan. Read it beside the surrounding concepts, and notice how the author's reasoning qualifies the conclusion.

### Chapter 2 Session 1 — Jurisprudential conditions for a peace treaty (armistice)

Mutahhari then turns to the jurisprudence of peace/truce, quoting Muhaqqiq's Shara'i' al-Islam: a truce with non-Muslims is permissible only if it serves Muslim interests — e.g., the Muslims are currently too few or weak to win, they need time to gather strength or allies, or the pause in hostilities is likely to lead the other side toward Islam — and even then it must run for a fixed, specified term rather than an indefinite one. If Muslims are strong enough to prevail and gain nothing from a truce, a truce is not permitted; this framework is what Mutahhari will apply to evaluate al-Hassan's treaty.

This concept belongs to the chapter's larger account of Imam Hasan's pacifism and the jurisprudence of peace. ### Chapter 2 Session 1 — The Hudaybiyyah precedent

Mutahhari retells the Hudaybiyyah episode at length as the model for al-Hassan's later peace: the Prophet marched to Mecca unarmed as a pilgrim, was blocked by the Quraysh, and accepted an apparently one-sided ten-year truce (including a clause requiring the return of any Muslim who fled to Medina, but not vice versa) purely to secure freedom to preach — a decision many companions, including 'Umar, initially resented. Within less than two years, this peace produced far more conversions than the previous two decades of conflict had, vindicating the Prophet's judgment and establishing that a costly-looking peace can be the strategically sound choice.

### Chapter 2 Session 1 — Q&A: does citing jurisprudence beg the question, and is Sunni fiqh on jihad different?

In the Q&A, a listener presses Mutahhari on whether it is circular to judge al-Hassan's conduct by a jurisprudence that is itself built on the Imams' precedents; Mutahhari clarifies he is testing whether the jurisprudence as a whole is internally logical, not claiming al-Hassan consciously followed later fiqh manuals. Asked whether Sunni jurisprudence differs on jihad, he says the substantive rules are close, with Shi'ah fiqh's distinguishing feature being its requirement of an imam's authorization for offensive jihad.

## Bringing the thread together

Peace is not presented as an automatic good regardless of circumstance, nor as a sign of weakness. The source examines the circumstances, the available choices, and the moral limits that determine whether restraint protects the community or merely postpones a greater injustice.

As you listen again, keep the chapter's sequence in view. The historical setting explains the pressure under which a decision was made; the moral principle explains how that decision should be judged; and the consequences show why the distinction matters. Do not collapse these three stages into one. A strong reading should be able to explain each stage in its own words and show how the source connects them.
