# Listen & Learn — Chapter 4

## Narration
The end of Umayyad rule created an extraordinary political opening. Opposition was widespread, and many people were looking for a new direction. Yet Imam al-Sadiq did not simply seize the moment and enter the political struggle.

Mutahhari asks why.

The answer begins with a warning about political movements that borrow the language of religion. The Abbasid organization mobilized support through slogans connected to the Household of the Prophet, while keeping its own succession plans deliberately vague.

Two important operators, Abu Muslim and Abu Salmah, helped organize the movement. Near the transition of power, Abu Salmah secretly sent parallel offers to Imam al-Sadiq and 'Abd Allah al-Mahd, suggesting that the caliphate could be redirected toward the family of Abu Talib.

Imam al-Sadiq did not treat the offer as an opportunity to be grabbed. He burned the letter.

The point is not simply that he rejected a political invitation. He recognized that the people making the offer had their own interests, and that apparent support for the Ahl al-Bayt did not necessarily mean genuine loyalty to their principles.

At the Abwa' gathering, Muhammad Nafs al-Zakiyyah was presented as a possible leader, and some even connected him with the promised Mahdi. Imam al-Sadiq refused to confuse a political claim with that religious concept. Yet he also made an important distinction: if the movement genuinely stood for enjoining good, resisting oppression and serving the right, that was a different matter.

## Deep Reading insight
The lesson is political discernment. Religious language does not automatically make a political project religious.

A mature reader asks: Who is making the claim? What do they actually want? What conditions exist? And what would my participation make possible?

## Reflection
When a cause uses language you deeply value, notice whether you evaluate the cause more generously than you would evaluate an ordinary political proposal.
