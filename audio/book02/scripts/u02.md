# Listen & Learn — Chapter 1: 'Ali's Struggles

## Narration
This chapter presents the struggles of Imam 'Ali as a lesson in the difference between moral clarity and political ease. Mutahhari begins with 'Ali's reluctance to accept the caliphate after the assassination of 'Uthman. Although people pressed him to take leadership, 'Ali recognized that the community was fractured and that accepting office would mean inheriting a crisis rather than receiving an uncontested mandate. His hesitation is important: leadership is not simply an honor to be claimed; it is a responsibility whose conditions and consequences must be understood.

The assassination had poisoned the political environment. 'Ali had tried to mediate between 'Uthman and those who opposed his policies, while disagreeing with favoritism and the concentration of authority among relatives. After the killing, many people wanted a new leader but did not necessarily want the justice that leadership would require. Mutahhari highlights this contradiction: people may demand principled government in public and then resist when principles remove advantages they have come to expect.

One of the chapter's central examples is 'Ali's refusal to distribute public wealth through special privileges for influential people. He would not purchase political support by treating public resources as private gifts. This made him morally consistent but politically vulnerable. Mutahhari's point is not that justice is a poor ideal; it is that justice can be difficult to sustain when a society has grown accustomed to unequal treatment. A leader who refuses to buy loyalty must be prepared for opposition from those who benefit from favoritism.

The chapter then turns to the Kharijites and asks why they became such a serious problem. Mutahhari contrasts the Prophet's long period of training a small community in Mecca with the situation 'Ali faced after the expansion of the Muslim community. Many new converts had entered a large political and military movement without the same depth of formation. Religious language and outward zeal could therefore coexist with limited judgment. The lesson is not that new believers are necessarily insincere; it is that commitment without knowledge, patience and disciplined interpretation can become dangerous.

At Siffin, when Mu'awiyah's side faced military pressure, Qur'ans were raised on spear-points. The gesture appealed to the religious conscience of people in 'Ali's army and created pressure to stop the fighting and accept arbitration. Mutahhari interprets this episode as a political stratagem that exploited sincere but insufficiently trained religious sentiment. A sacred symbol can be used to manipulate people if they respond to its outward appearance without examining the purpose and circumstances in which it is invoked.

The demand for arbitration then produced another crisis. Some members of 'Ali's army insisted on arbitration and rejected the people he proposed, pressing instead for Abu Musa al-Ash'ari. When the process failed to deliver the outcome they expected, some of those who had demanded it turned against 'Ali and condemned acceptance of arbitration as disbelief. Mutahhari uses this reversal to expose a recurring danger: people can force a decision under pressure and later blame the leader for the decision they themselves demanded.

Before the Kharijites moved into armed revolt, 'Ali tolerated their opposition for a considerable period. According to the chapter, he did not simply strip them of their share in public resources or suppress them merely because they criticized him. This distinction matters. Political disagreement and even offensive speech are not automatically the same as armed violence. A just leader must distinguish between opposition that can be endured and actions that threaten people's safety.

The Kharijites eventually formalized their break into doctrines that condemned 'Ali, Mu'awiyah and others connected with arbitration. Their religious certainty became rigid enough to justify violence against people who disagreed with them. Mutahhari emphasizes the contrast between their bravery and worship on the one hand, and their ignorance and harsh judgment on the other. Sincerity, courage and intense ritual practice do not guarantee sound understanding. When devotion is separated from knowledge and humility, it can be turned into a weapon.

At Nahrawan, 'Ali first attempted to address the Kharijites through discussion and negotiation. Ibn 'Abbas was sent to speak with them, and 'Ali himself appealed to them. The chapter stresses the distinction between those who abandoned rebellion and those who persisted in violence. His final approach was not simply indiscriminate punishment; it followed attempts to prevent bloodshed and a recognition that armed violence had become a real threat.

Mutahhari also recounts an anecdote about a rumor concerning the poet Muhammad Iqbal and Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq. The story illustrates how an inaccurate report can spread when people repeat an allegation without checking the text or context. This is presented as a modern example of a Kharijite-like habit of judgment: strong emotion and apparent piety can make people more confident, not more careful. The lesson is to verify claims before condemning a person, especially when the claim concerns religion or another person's honor.

The chapter ends with the assassination plot involving Ibn Muljam and the final instructions attributed to 'Ali. Mutahhari distinguishes the political danger posed by hypocrisy and the danger posed by the Kharijites. 'Ali's reported instruction not to kill the Kharijites merely for being Kharijites, unless they themselves resorted to violence, reinforces the chapter's central distinction: a person's belief or criticism is not by itself a license for bloodshed. Conduct, evidence and actual aggression matter.

## Deep Reading insight
The chapter repeatedly separates outward religious intensity from moral and intellectual maturity. A person may be courageous, devout and convinced of being right while still misunderstanding evidence or acting unjustly. The remedy is not to abandon conviction; it is to join conviction with knowledge, patience, verification and restraint.

The same pattern appears in leadership. Justice may cost popularity, and a leader may be blamed by people who demanded one decision and later regret it. That does not make every unpopular decision correct. It means that decisions must be judged by their reasons, context and consequences—not merely by how loudly people praise or condemn them afterward.

## Reflection
Think of a recent claim you accepted because it sounded religious, urgent or emotionally convincing. Did you check the original source and its context? What would change if you delayed judgment until you had verified the evidence?

## Transition
The next chapter applies this broader problem of context to the apparent difference between Imam al-Hassan's peace and Imam al-Husayn's uprising. Mutahhari asks us to judge each Imam's action in light of the circumstances, rather than turning different strategies into contradictory moral identities.

## A closer walk through the source

### 'Ali's reluctance to accept the caliphate

'Ali initially refused the caliphate after 'Uthman's assassination despite people insisting on swearing allegiance to him, saying (Nahj al-Balaghah, Sermon 91): 'Leave me and seek someone else. We are facing a matter that has several sides and colors, which neither hearts can bear nor intelligence fathom... You should know that if I respond to you, I will lead you as I know how (not how you want me to)... At present, I would rather stay a minister than to become a chief.' Mutahhari reads this as 'Ali foreseeing the immense problems that would in fact later materialize during his caliphate.

The important question is not only what happened, but what options were available and what each option would have meant for the people involved. The source invites us to weigh intention, practical consequence, and principle together rather than treating any one of them as sufficient by itself.

### 'Uthman's assassination and the problem of hypocrisy

'Ali inherited a caliphate poisoned by 'Uthman's assassination. 'Ali had acted as mediator between 'Uthman and the revolutionaries, disagreeing with 'Uthman's favoritism toward relatives but also opposing 'opening the door' to killing a caliph. Mu'awiyah covertly worked to escalate the unrest that led to 'Uthman's murder, then exploited it for his own political advantage — sending 'Uthman's blood-stained shirt and his wife's severed fingers to Damascus, hanging them up, and inciting the people to avenge 'Uthman's blood against 'Ali. Unlike the Prophet's opponents (open polytheists under the slogan 'Long Live Hubal'), 'Ali faced an intelligent, non-religious group that pretended to follow Islam while pursuing anti-Islamic aims — using Qur'an 17:33 ('whosoever is killed unjustly, we have given his heir authority') as a deceptive slogan despite Mu'awiyah having no real kinship claim to 'Uthman's blood. This led directly to the battles of Jamal and Siffin.

### Inflexibility in justice and bluntness in politics

'Ali refused to allocate special subsidies to influential people (a custom that had crept in after the Prophet's death), saying: 'Are you asking me to gain victory and success in politics at the price of oppression and destroying the rights of powerless people?! I swear upon the Almighty, as long as there is day and night in this world, I will not do such a thing.' His bluntness and honesty in politics also drew criticism from his own supporters, who argued 'some dishonesty and deception is necessary' in politics and unfavorably compared him to the more 'tactful' Mu'awiyah. 'Ali responded (Sermon 199) that Mu'awiyah is not more cunning than him — 'he is deceitful... If Allah did not consider deception as his enemy, you would have seen that 'Ali would have been the most cunning of all people' — but that such deceitfulness is immoral, evil, wicked and blasphemous.

This concept belongs to the chapter's larger account of Ali's struggles and the Khawarij. It should not be heard as an isolated anecdote: it helps establish the circumstances in which the next moral judgment becomes intelligible. Ask what evidence is being presented, which principle the author applies to it, and what conclusion follows.

### Why the Kharijites were 'Ali's fundamental problem: the Prophet's 13-year training period vs. 'Ali's untrained converts

Mutahhari explains the structural cause of the Kharijite problem. During his 13 years in Mecca, the Prophet trained a small, deeply grounded core group before any war was permitted — even under torture, he refused permission for jihad, consenting only to emigration. This created a group (~1,000 by the time of migration) who deeply understood Islam's essence, not just its outward form. Under the later caliphs, especially 'Uthman's, this training method was neglected in favor of rapid conquest, producing a new class of Muslims familiar only with Islam's outward 'crust' — exemplified by people with calloused foreheads from excessive prostration, described by Ibn 'Abbas after visiting the future rebels: 'Their foreheads are wounded because of excessive prostrations; they have hands that have calluses like the knee of a camel... Most manifest are their resolute and indomitable faces.' 'Ali described this class bluntly: 'They are a people who are rough, remorseless, tough, hard-hearted, rude, but with inferior, slavish characters and spirits... They do not know what the Qur'an is. They do not understand the meaning of the Qur'an.'

### The Qur'ans-on-spears stratagem at Siffin

At Siffin, when Mu'awiyah and 'Amr ibn al-'As realized they were losing, they ordered Qur'ans raised on spear-points, provoking the untrained pietist faction within 'Ali's own army to refuse to keep fighting ('We shall not fight the Qur'an'). 'Ali objected: 'They have not brought forward the Qur'an itself but its papers and cover so that they can rise up again against this very Qur'an... I am your imam. I am your speaking Qur'an.' The faction accused 'Ali of having his own ambitions and refused to continue. When Malik al-Ashtar was on the verge of victory, they threatened to kill 'Ali himself unless Malik was recalled (they numbered about 20,000); 'Ali sent word for Malik to return.

### Arbitration, Abu Musa al-Ash'ari, and the birth of Kharijite doctrine

The faction insisted on arbitration and rejected 'Ali's proposed arbitrators (Ibn 'Abbas, then Malik al-Ashtar), instead forcing Abu Musa al-Ash'ari on him — a former governor 'Ali had removed, and 'in his heart an enemy of 'Ali.' Abu Musa was outmaneuvered by 'Amr ibn al-'As in the arbitration. Realizing they had been tricked, the faction did not conclude that they erred in halting the fight or in choosing Abu Musa; instead they declared that appointing any human arbitrator was itself blasphemy, citing Qur'an 6:57 ('The judgment belongs to none but Allah'), and demanded 'Ali repent alongside them for having become 'a non-believer like us.' 'Ali refused: 'Arbitration is no blasphemy. You do not understand the meaning of the verse... I shall never confess to a sin I have never committed.' They split from him, forming the Kharijite sect ('the rebels against 'Ali').

### 'Ali's tolerance of the Kharijites before their armed revolt

'Ali tolerated the Kharijites' open disrespect for a long period before they turned to armed insurrection: he never cut their share of the treasury or restricted their freedom. When a Kharijite in the crowd cursed him after a masterful answer to a question ('May Allah kill him, how knowledgeable he is'), 'Ali stopped his companions from retaliating: 'Leave him be. He cursed me. The most you can do to him is to curse him. Leave him alone.' During prayer, a Kharijite named Ibn al-Kawwab repeatedly interrupted 'Ali by reciting Qur'an 39:65 (implying his deeds were nullified by 'polytheism'); 'Ali, obeying 7:204 ('when the Qur'an is recited, listen and keep silent'), stayed silent each time, then on the third or fourth repetition recited 30:60 ('let not those who have no certitude make you unstable') and continued his prayer without further reaction.

### The Kharijite sect's formal doctrines

The Kharijites formalized their break into a religious sect with explicit doctrines: (1) 'Uthman, 'Ali, Mu'awiyah, and all who accepted arbitration are non-believers, except those who repented; (2) 'enjoining good and forbidding evil' is obligatory and unconditional — one must rise against any unlawful or cruel ruler even believing the rising will fail; (3) faith is inseparable from action — a single major sin (lying, drinking, backbiting) expels a person from Islam entirely. The practical result: they considered themselves the only true Muslims, declared others' meat forbidden and intermarriage forbidden, and — most extremely — considered killing the women and children of non-Kharijites permissible. They killed a passing companion of the Prophet and his pregnant wife (ripping open her stomach) for refusing to disown 'Ali, and rebuked a man for eating a single date from an unattended garden as 'intruding on your Muslim brother's wealth.'

### Nahrawan: negotiation, the banner, and the battle

When the Kharijites' actions escalated from words to violence, 'Ali confronted them at Nahrawan. Ibn 'Abbas was sent first but achieved nothing. 'Ali then spoke to them himself; of roughly 12,000, 8,000 repented under the protective banner 'Ali raised, and 4,000 refused and were killed in battle, with only about 10 survivors — including 'Abd al-Rahman ibn Muljam, 'Ali's future assassin. 'Ali's own verdict on the act (Sermon 92): 'It was I and I alone who removed the eye of this revolt. No one save me could have stopped them with his sword.' He explained that no ordinary believer would have had the nerve to draw a sword against people who appeared more pious and ascetic than himself — 'their hands would shiver, their heart would tremble' — and that only an unbeliever, acting for the wrong reasons, could otherwise have done it. He used the image of kalab (rabies): the Kharijites were like rabid dogs whose disease (fanatical false piety) would spread to whoever they bit, so that eradication, though tragic, was necessary to save the wider body of Islam from 'rigidity, petrifaction, idiocy and ignorance.'

### Characteristics of the Kharijites: bravery, worship, ignorance

Mutahhari lists their distinguishing traits: extraordinary bravery and devotion to their cause; excessive, dogmatic worship (the source of their apparent piety and the reason no ordinary Muslim dared oppose them); and above all ignorance and lack of real Islamic knowledge, which 'Ali called their defining vice: 'You are the worst of people... because you are spears in the hands of the devil. Satan places you in his bow instead of his arrows and crushes his targets with you.' Mutahhari notes that Mu'awiyah and 'Amr ibn al-'As — genuine hypocrites who privately respected 'Ali (Mu'awiyah reportedly wept when 'Ali was later described to him, saying 'Time can never again bring a person like 'Ali') — deliberately used ignorant pietist factions as tools to achieve their political ends, a pattern Mutahhari says recurs 'in various guises' throughout history.

### The Iqbal/Ja'far-Sadiq rumor as a modern parable of Kharijite-style ignorance

Mutahhari recounts a contemporary anecdote: a rumor spread that the poet Muhammad Iqbal had cursed Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq in his writing, based on a couplet actually condemning two historical traitors coincidentally also named Ja'far (of Bengal) and Sadiq (of Deccan) who betrayed Muslim rulers to British colonizers. Mutahhari traces the rumor to its source and shows it to be a complete misreading, using it as a direct parallel to Kharijite-style thinking: declaring people non-believers or heretics based on surface impressions without careful investigation. He urges: 'Investigate! Investigate... then say whatever you want, but do not say anything before you have done your research,' explicitly invoking the Nahrawan Kharijites as the cautionary example ('Do not be like the Nahrawan Kharijites').

### Ibn Muljam, Qatam, and the assassination plot

'Abd al-Rahman ibn Muljam was one of nine ascetic Kharijites who vowed in Mecca that 'Ali, Mu'awiyah and 'Amr ibn al-'As were the causes of all the Muslim world's strife, and drew lots to assassinate each; Ibn Muljam was assigned 'Ali. In Kufah he fell in love with a Kharijite woman, Qatam, whose father and brother 'Ali had killed at Nahrawan. She agreed to marry him only on the condition — as her fourth and final dowry demand, after money, a slave boy, and a slave girl — that he kill 'Ali: 'If you seek union with me, you must kill 'Ali. If you live, you will gain what you seek, and if you die, then nothing.' He composed verses declaring no dowry in the world was as exalted as 'Ali's blood. The date chosen was the night before the 19th of Ramadan, deliberately selected — per Ibn Abi al-Hadid — because the Kharijites believed killing 'Ali on the Night of Qadr (Laylat al-Qadr) would earn greater heavenly reward.

### 'Ali's final instruction on the Kharijites and his death

On his deathbed, 'Ali distinguished the danger of Mu'awiyah's hypocrites from that of the Kharijites and instructed his followers: 'After me, do not kill the Kharijites anymore, because whoever seeks truth and commits a mistake is not the same as the one who seeks falsehood from the beginning and has reached it' — since killing them further would only serve Mu'awiyah's interests, not truth and justice. When Ibn Muljam was captured and brought before the dying 'Ali, 'Ali asked him, 'Why did you do such a thing? Was I a bad Imam for you?' Ibn Muljam, unrepentant, said he had vowed to kill the worst of God's creatures with that sword; 'Ali replied that his prayer had indeed been answered, since he himself would be killed by that same sword. 'Ali died on the night of the 21st of Ramadan and was buried in secret by his closest family, his grave's location concealed for about a hundred years — out of fear the Kharijites would desecrate it — until Imam al-Sadiq revealed it publicly after the Umayyad dynasty's fall.

## Bringing the thread together

The chapter's political narrative must be read alongside its moral standard. The author repeatedly contrasts the difficulty of governing justly with the temptation to win by expediency, and asks what happens when a slogan is separated from sound interpretation.

As you listen again, keep the chapter's sequence in view. The historical setting explains the pressure under which a decision was made; the moral principle explains how that decision should be judged; and the consequences show why the distinction matters. Do not collapse these three stages into one. A strong reading should be able to explain each stage in its own words and show how the source connects them.
