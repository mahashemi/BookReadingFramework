# Listen & Learn — Differences Between People — The Four Types

## Narration

This section distinguishes different patterns of human attraction and repulsion. The purpose of the classification is not to label people casually, but to help us recognize when a relationship supports growth, when it is shallow, and when an apparent virtue or dislike is actually misplaced.

We will move through the concepts in sequence. Listen for the way each point contributes to the larger argument, and for the difference between what the source reports and what it asks the reader to infer.

### Type 1 — attracts nothing, repels nothing

Type 1 — attracts nothing, repels nothing. Nobody's friend, nobody's enemy. Goes among men 'just as if a slab of rock were to be among them'. Produces no effect, positive or negative — and 'positive' here covers wickedness as well as virtue. Compared to a sheep, fed only because its meat will be eaten. Man needs to love and be loved, and also to hate and be hated. Verdict: hollow, vacuous, worthless.

A practical implication follows: when we face a similar choice, we should ask what is actually at stake, whose interests are being served, and whether our response is guided by knowledge rather than impulse. In particular, the lesson is strongest when it becomes a standard for self-examination instead of a reason to judge others from a distance.

### Type 2 — attracts all, repels none

Type 2 — attracts all, repels none. The Zamzam/cremation poem: live so Muslims wash you with Zamzam water and Hindus cremate you. Often mistaken for 'good character' or 'being sociable'. But impossible for anyone with a real aim, unless he is a hypocrite and double-faced. Men do not all think alike — there are just and unjust, equitable and despotic. Only the dissimulator pleases everyone.

The argument here does more than preserve a detail. It gives us a criterion for judging conduct: we must look at the reason behind an action, the principle being claimed, and the effect that follows. In particular, the lesson is strongest when it becomes a standard for self-examination instead of a reason to judge others from a distance.

### Critique of 'love-only' philosophies: some Christians and Hindus hold the perfect man has only attraction

Critique of 'love-only' philosophies: some Christians and Hindus hold the perfect man has only attraction. Mutahhari: it is not enough to be a man of love, one must be a man with a path — as Gandhi said, 'This is my religion.' Love must coincide with reality; reality has a path; a path creates enemies.

Notice the movement from an example to a general lesson. The historical detail matters because it tests an idea against real choices, where motives, pressure, and consequences cannot be separated. In particular, attraction is not complete until it changes the direction of the self; the relationship should cultivate virtues rather than stop at admiration.

### Qur'anic balance: the Prophet is rahmatan li'l-'alamin (21:107) — mercy to all beings, even the dangerous enemy

Qur'anic balance: the Prophet is rahmatan li'l-'alamin (21:107) — mercy to all beings, even the dangerous enemy. But love does not mean approving everyone's inclinations; that is hypocrisy. Rational love seeks the good of mankind as a whole, not one individual or group — and many things that benefit an individual harm society.

This is a useful place to pause. A slogan may sound convincing on its own, but the fuller question is whether the interpretation is sound and whether the action that follows is just, proportionate, and faithful to the principle. In particular, the moral force of this episode depends on discernment: intention matters, but so do knowledge, method, and the consequences of action.

### Ibn Muljam: 'Ali knew he was a dangerous enemy; others urged him to act

Ibn Muljam: 'Ali knew he was a dangerous enemy; others urged him to act. 'Should I punish before the crime? He is to murder me, not I him.' — I want him to live; he wants to kill me.

The source invites us to reason rather than merely react. Keep the claim, the evidence offered for it, and the author's conclusion distinct; that makes the argument easier to understand and to evaluate fairly. In particular, the lesson is strongest when it becomes a standard for self-examination instead of a reason to judge others from a distance.

### Type 3 — repels all, attracts none

Type 3 — repels all, attracts none. Also deficient: since there are always some good people, a man with no friends must have the fault in himself. Such people are 'sour throughout', even in their villainy.

A practical implication follows: when we face a similar choice, we should ask what is actually at stake, whose interests are being served, and whether our response is guided by knowledge rather than impulse. ### Type 4 — attracts AND repels

Type 4 — attracts AND repels. People travelling a path. Strength varies: at maximum, friends give their souls and enemies give their lives, and the effect persists centuries after death. But — crucially — this is not proof of virtue. Genghis Khan, Hajjaj and Mu'awiyah all had both powers strongly. What matters is who is attracted and who repelled: these are 'a clear proof of the essence of such a person'.

## Deep Reading Insight

A classification is useful only when it sharpens judgment. The aim is not to sort people into boxes, but to discover whether our attachments and dislikes are helping us move toward truth and virtue.
