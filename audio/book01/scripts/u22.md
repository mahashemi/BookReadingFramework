### THE MU'TAZILAH

THE MU'TAZILAH. Arose at the beginning of the second century AH, in the wake of the debate over whether committing great sins makes one a disbeliever — 'naturally their coming into existence was connected with the Kharijites'. They wanted 'a degree of free thinking' and 'to create an intellectual life'; though lacking a scientific basis, they investigated Islamic problems fairly freely, critically evaluated ahadith, and followed only researched conclusions. They stood against those who based everything on ahadith and against the exoterists, who recognised only the outward meaning, had nothing to do with the spirit or inner meaning of Qur'an and hadith, and denied that clear judgements follow from the intellect.

A practical implication follows: when we face a similar choice, we should ask what is actually at stake, whose interests are being served, and whether our response is guided by knowledge rather than impulse. ### THE ASH'ARITES

THE ASH'ARITES. After about one and a half centuries of the Mu'tazili school's ups and downs, the Ash'arites arose and 'once again the value of sheer intellectual thinking and reflexion and the reckonings of pure metaphysics were denied'. They held that Muslims must believe the exoteric meanings of traditional explanations and not think or reflect upon deeper meanings; 'every kind of question and answer, or why and wherefore, was an innovation for them'. Ahmad ibn Hanbal, one of the four Sunni Imams, opposed Mu'tazili thinking so strongly that he went to prison and was tortured for it, yet maintained his opposition.

### THE AKHBARI SCHOOL

THE AKHBARI SCHOOL. 'Also a kind of dissociation of intellection and religion' — a Shi'ah school of jurists, at its height in the eleventh and twelfth centuries AH (18th CE). They had much in common with the Sunni exoterics and traditionists, following the same method of deriving laws and differing only in which ahadith they chose. They 'completely shut down the work of the intellect', denied any probative value to intellectual perception in deriving rulings, considered following the intellect absolutely forbidden, and campaigned against the Usulis. They held the only proofs were Qur'an and sunnah — but since the Qur'an's probative force came only through hadith exegesis, they 'virtually disregarded the Qur'an as a source of proof and only recognized the outward meaning of ahadith to be trustworthy'.

## Deep Reading Insight

Ideas can survive under new names and in new communities. This makes self-examination necessary: a person may oppose a historical movement while unconsciously repeating its habits of thought.
