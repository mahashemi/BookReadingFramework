# Listen & Learn — Love for 'Ali in the Qur'an and Sunnah

## Narration

This section brings the discussion to love for Ali in the Qur'an and the Prophetic tradition. The narration must distinguish the source's claims and evidence from our explanatory commentary, while making clear why the author treats love as a bond with moral consequences.

### The set-up: love for the pure ones has been shown to be a means of reform, not an end

The set-up: love for the pure ones has been shown to be a means of reform, not an end. Now: has Islam designated someone we should love?

The argument here does more than preserve a detail. It gives us a criterion for judging conduct: we must look at the reason behind an action, the principle being claimed, and the effect that follows. Attraction is not complete until it changes the direction of the self; the relationship should cultivate virtues rather than stop at admiration.

The wage verse (42:23): all previous prophets said 'we ask no wage from people; our reward is from God' — but the Seal of the Prophets is told: 'Say: I do not ask of you a wage for this, except love for (my) relatives (al-mawaddah fi'l-qurba).' Why did he alone ask a wage?

Notice the movement from an example to a general lesson. The historical detail matters because it tests an idea against real choices, where motives, pressure, and consequences cannot be separated. The lesson is strongest when it becomes a standard for self-examination instead of a reason to judge others from a distance.

### The Qur'an answers itself (34:47): 'Say: I have asked no wage of you; that shall be yours

The Qur'an answers itself (34:47): 'Say: I have asked no wage of you; that shall be yours. My wage falls only upon God.' I.e. the 'wage' accrues to you, not to me — this friendship is 'a halter for your own perfection and reformation'. It is in fact another good he recommends, since the Household are people 'who do not gather round defilement, and whose hems are clean and pure'. Love for them 'brings no other result apart from obedience to the truth and adherence to virtues, and it is friendship for them which transmutes and perfects like the elixir'.

A practical test follows from this point. A slogan may sound convincing on its own, but the fuller question is whether the interpretation is sound and whether the action that follows is just, proportionate, and faithful to the principle. The lesson is strongest when it becomes a standard for self-examination instead of a reason to judge others from a distance.

### Fakhr al-Din al-Razi (a Sunni authority) is quoted at length: Zamakhshari in al-Kashshaf relates that when the verse came down they asked 'W

Fakhr al-Din al-Razi (a Sunni authority) is quoted at length: Zamakhshari in al-Kashshaf relates that when the verse came down they asked 'Who are the relatives whose love is due?' — the Prophet said: ''Ali and Fatimah and their sons.' Razi reasons: (1) the verse itself; (2) the Prophet demonstrably loved Fatimah ('Fatimah is a part of my body; what harms her harms me'), and 'Ali and the Hasanayn, by mutawatir traditions — therefore friendship for them is obligatory on all the community, since the Qur'an commands 'follow him' (7:158) and 'you have a good example in Allah's Messenger' (33:21).

A practical implication follows: when we face a similar choice, we should ask what is actually at stake, whose interests are being served, and whether our response is guided by knowledge rather than impulse. The lesson is strongest when it becomes a standard for self-examination instead of a reason to judge others from a distance.

### Important footnote: the Prophet's love for them had no personal aspect — not merely because they were his children/grandchildren

Important footnote: the Prophet's love for them had no personal aspect — not merely because they were his children/grandchildren. He loved them because they were exemplary and God loved them; he had other children whom he did not love to this extent and to whom the community owes no such obligation.

Traditions on loving 'Ali: Ibn al-Athir (Usd al-Ghabah): 'O 'Ali, God has embellished you… resignation from the world… On you has been bestowed the love of the wretched… Content is he who loves you and is a true friend to you; woe betides he who shows enmity towards you and lies about you.' — as-Suyuti: 'Love of 'Ali is faith, and enmity towards him is sedition.' — Abu Na'im (Hilyat al-Awliya'): 'Shall I guide you to something which, if you grasp it after me, you will never go astray? The chicken tradition (Anas ibn Malik): Umm Ayman brought a cooked chicken; the Prophet prayed, 'O God! Send the best of Thy slaves that he may share with me in eating this chicken.' 'Ali knocked three times; Anas, hoping for an Ansari, twice turned him away saying the Prophet was busy. The third time the Prophet said: 'Anas, go and open the door and bring him in. You are not the first person to love your own people; that are not one of the Ansar.' (Related in more than eighty narrations in Sunni texts.)

## Deep Reading Insight

A claim grounded in scripture and tradition should be read with attention to both its evidence and its ethical implications. The question is not only what love is affirmed, but what kind of fidelity it calls forth.
