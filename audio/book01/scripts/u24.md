# Listen & Learn — ‘Ali, the True Imam and Leader

## Narration

The book closes by bringing its two central themes together: attraction and repulsion. Mutahhari asks readers to learn from the whole of ‘Ali’s character and conduct, not merely to admire his name or repeat a slogan about loyalty.

The closing section is brief, and the author acknowledges that limitation. Its purpose is to leave the reader with a practical test: do we recognize truth and moral character beneath outward appearances, and do our own choices reflect the same discernment we praise in ‘Ali?

## A closer walk through the source

### The closing section returns to ‘Ali as a model of instruction and leadership

Mutahhari opens the conclusion by saying that ‘Ali’s history, habits, character, behaviour, and words all offer instruction. The point is broader than learning a few memorable sayings. A person’s moral example appears in the pattern of a life: how principles shape conduct, how decisions are made, and what kind of response they produce in others.

That makes the ending a return to the book’s purpose. The reader is not asked only to admire ‘Ali from a distance, but to examine what his example teaches about leadership and personal responsibility.

### Attraction and repulsion both teach

Mutahhari argues that ‘Ali’s power of attraction teaches, and so does his power of repulsion. The closing passage connects this idea with the devotional language of loving those aligned with the Ahl al-Bayt and distancing oneself from what they rejected. In the book’s reasoning, this is not simply a matter of belonging to a group; it is a question of moral judgment.

Neither attraction nor repulsion is automatically virtuous. People can be drawn toward falsehood, and they can reject what is good. The important question is what the attraction or rejection is responding to, and whether that response is guided by truth.

### The author acknowledges the limits of his brief treatment

Mutahhari openly describes his account as only an indication of ‘Ali’s powers of attraction and repulsion, and he says that the treatment of repulsion is especially brief. That qualification helps readers understand the scope of the chapter. It offers a focused conclusion, not a claim to have exhausted ‘Ali’s character or every detail of his history.

A careful reader should preserve that distinction. The author’s central criterion can be discussed and applied, while the limits of this short treatment should still be acknowledged.

### The two groups ‘Ali strongly repelled

Mutahhari identifies two broad groups that ‘Ali strongly repelled: artful hypocrites and ascetics whose outward piety was joined to ignorance. This connects the chapter’s discussion of political hypocrisy with its treatment of the Khawarij, whose visible devotion did not guarantee sound understanding of religion.

The shared problem is a gap between outward appearance and inner reality. In one case, religious language can conceal a self-serving purpose; in the other, intense devotion can coexist with a serious failure of understanding. The source asks readers to examine both conduct and judgment rather than taking appearances as decisive evidence.

### Do not be deceived by hypocrisy

Mutahhari draws a direct lesson for those who claim to follow ‘Ali: they should remain alert to hypocrisy and not be deceived by people who present themselves as religious while acting against the principles they invoke. This is not a command to assume bad faith in everyone. It is a warning not to treat religious vocabulary, public piety, or group identity as sufficient proof of sincerity.

The test is whether a person’s actions and methods serve the truth they claim to defend. A slogan may be correct while the purpose behind its use is not; a religious appearance may impress observers while concealing conduct that contradicts the stated ideal.

### Look beyond outward appearances

The conclusion urges readers to keep their vision clear and to look beyond outward forms. Mutahhari presents this as a particular concern for a community that may be misled when it values appearances more than truthful assessment of character and conduct.

The book therefore returns its central theme to the reader. Attraction and repulsion should follow moral insight, not habit, faction, or surface impression. The question is not only whom ‘Ali attracted or repelled, but whether we have learned to recognize the reasons behind those responses.

## Deep Reading Insight

A closing summary can be short without being shallow. Here, the key is the distinction between outward identity and the truth of a person’s conduct. The author’s final lesson is not simply “follow the right person”; it is to develop the discernment needed to judge claims, methods, and character by truth.

## Reflection

Think of a time when a familiar name, religious appearance, or persuasive slogan influenced your judgment. What evidence did you use to decide whether the person’s conduct matched the principle they claimed to represent? How could you apply the same test to your own behaviour before using it to judge others?

## Transition

This completes the book’s sequence of learning units. As you review the whole work, connect its discussions of attraction, repulsion, truth, and moral responsibility—and distinguish the author’s historical examples from the principles he draws from them.
