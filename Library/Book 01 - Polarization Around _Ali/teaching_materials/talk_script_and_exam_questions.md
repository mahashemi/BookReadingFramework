# Speaking Script & Exam Questions
### This Session's Material — *The Power of Attraction in 'Ali*
Companion to the outline. This is the "what to actually say" version of each section — full explanatory text — followed by **Key Questions to Remember** for exam-prep. Previous session's material is condensed into a quick recap block only.

---

## 0. Recap of Previous Session (speak briefly, ~5 min)

**What to say:**
"Quick recap before we move on. Last session we established that attraction and repulsion is a real law running through all of creation — not just human feeling, but something physicists say governs every atom, and something the ancients only partly understood through cases like the magnet and iron. We brought that down to human relationships, where Mutahhari argues attraction comes from either genuine resemblance — like Rumi's raven and stork, who turn out to both be lame in one leg — or real opposition. From there we built out four types of people: the ones who attract nothing and repel nothing, like a slab of rock; the ones who attract everyone and repel no one, which usually means hypocrisy, since real conviction always creates some friction; the ones who repel everyone and attract no one; and the ones — like 'Ali — who genuinely attract and repel, both strongly. We were careful to note that having both powers strongly isn't proof of virtue by itself — even tyrants like Genghis Khan had it. What matters is *who* is drawn in and *who* is driven away. And we closed on 'Ali as 'one man with two powers,' whose attraction and repulsion were unmatched in any century. That's where today picks up — we're going to see exactly *how* that attraction actually worked across history, and what love itself does to a person."

**Key Questions to Remember (previous session):**
- What is the universal law of attraction, and how did ancient people's understanding differ from it?
- What are the four types of people, and which type does 'Ali belong to?
- Why is having strong attraction *and* repulsion not, by itself, proof of good character?

## 1. Opening — Hadiths from Al-Kāfi

**What to say:**
"Before we go further, I want to open with a principle from Al-Kāfi, because it sets up everything we'll see today. There's a hadith where Imam al-Sadiq (a.s.) is asked directly: is love and hate part of belief itself? His answer is striking — he says belief *is* nothing but love and hate, and he points to the Qur'an, where faith is described as something made *beloved* to the heart, and disbelief made *hateful* to it. The Prophet (s) goes even further — he tells his companions that the strongest handhold of belief isn't prayer alone, or fasting alone, or hajj alone — it's loving for the sake of Allah, hating for the sake of Allah, and aligning your loyalty and rejection accordingly. So today, as we watch 'Ali's attraction reach across centuries, and watch what real love does to the people around him, keep this principle in mind: love and hate aren't things to apologize for or balance away in Islam. They are acts of faith."

**Key Questions to Remember:**
- According to the hadith, what did Imam al-Sadiq say belief actually consists of?
- What did the Prophet identify as the *strongest handhold* of belief?

## 2. Powerful Attractions Across Centuries (Ch. 9)

**What to say:**
"So where does 'Ali fit in the picture of attraction we built last time? Mutahhari says no one in any century has had attraction and repulsion as strong as his. To explain how powerful this really is, he borrows a framework from *The Seal of the Prophets*: calls to humanity can be one-dimensional — they spread wide, then vanish; two-dimensional — they spread wide *and* last through time; or three-dimensional — they spread wide, last for centuries, *and* penetrate the very depth of the human heart. He says this three-dimensional category is unique to the prophets — and that 'Ali's power of attraction belongs to that same category.

To prove it, he gives us two stories that span centuries. First, Maytham at-Tammar — twenty years after 'Ali's death, under brutal Umayyad repression, when even speaking freely could get you killed, he spoke from his own crucifixion about 'Ali's virtues, drawing crowds until the authorities gagged him and eventually executed him. Second, Ibn as-Sikkit — a great scholar of Arabic literature, roughly two hundred years after 'Ali's death, tutoring the Caliph al-Mutawakkil's own children. When the caliph provocatively asked whether his sons were dearer to him than Hasan and Husayn, Ibn as-Sikkit answered that even 'Ali's slave Qanbar was dearer to him than the caliph and his sons — and had his tongue cut out for that honesty.

Mutahhari adds something important here too — he says that if 'Ali hadn't had this divine colouring, he would have been forgotten, just like every other historical champion of speech, knowledge, or the battlefield is eventually forgotten. But 'Ali didn't just survive his own death — in Mutahhari's words, 'he became more alive.' 'Ali himself seemed to know this, saying: 'after my place has been vacated and someone else has occupied it, you will know me.'

The point: this is love strong enough to override self-preservation, centuries after the person being loved has died. That's not something material self-interest can explain — it belongs to a different order of connection entirely."

**Key Questions to Remember:**
- What are the three "dimensions" of a call to humanity, according to *The Seal of the Prophets*? Which one does 'Ali's attraction belong to?
- How long after 'Ali's death did Maytham at-Tammar speak from his own crucifixion in praise of him?
- What did Ibn as-Sikkit say to Caliph al-Mutawakkil, and what did it cost him?
- What does Mutahhari mean when he says 'Ali "became more alive" after his death?

## 3. Shi'ism, the Religion of Love (Ch. 10)

**What to say:**
"Mutahhari now roots all of this in the Prophet's own words: 'Ali and his party — the Shi'ah — will be the triumphant ones. From this, he says, Shi'ism's foundation is love and devotion — taking 'Ali as one's friend *is* the way of love.

But here's the key nuance: this love was never about 'Ali being soft or lenient. He administered real punishments — lashes, even amputation, under Divine Law — and yet those he punished did *not* turn away from him. 'Ali himself explained why: he said that if he struck a true believer with his sword, it would never turn that believer into his enemy — and if he poured all the wealth of the world onto a hypocrite's head, it would never make the hypocrite love him. This was decreed by the Prophet himself: 'the believer will never be your enemy and the hypocrite will never love you.'

This makes 'Ali a kind of test — a standard for assaying human nature. A sound nature won't turn against him even under his sword; a diseased nature won't love him even after receiving favors. Mutahhari proves this with a striking story: a good man is punished by 'Ali, who cuts off the fingers of his hand for wrongdoing. A Kharijite named Ibn al-Kawwa' tries to use this to turn the man against 'Ali, asking mockingly who did this to him. Instead of resentment, the man launches into an extended, genuine tribute to 'Ali — ending with, 'Why should I not extol him, now that his friendship is mixed with flesh and blood? ... he did not cut off my hand except with a right that God has established.' This is love that survives punishment, because it's rooted in truth and justice, not personal comfort."

**Key Questions to Remember:**
- What Prophetic statement does Mutahhari use to root Shi'ism in the principle of love?
- According to 'Ali's own words, why will a hypocrite never love him no matter what he does for them?
- What does the story of the man whose hand was cut off demonstrate about the nature of real love for 'Ali?
- In what sense does Mutahhari describe 'Ali as a "standard" or "criterion" for human nature?

## 4. The Elixir of Love (Ch. 11)

**What to say:**
"Now Mutahhari turns philosophical. Persian poets called love 'the elixir' — borrowing from the alchemists, who believed in a mythical substance, the philosopher's stone, that could transmute one material into another. The poets said: love *is* that real elixir — it's the thing that actually transmutes human nature. As the text puts it, people are mines, like gold-mines and silver-mines — and love is what refines them.

He gives us a series of vivid images to prove it. Take the hen: alone, she's timid, fleeing from the slightest noise, not even standing up to a child. But give her chicks to protect, and she transforms — wings lowered in defense, clucking louder, attacking instead of fleeing. Or take two young people — before falling in love and starting a family, they think only of themselves. Afterward, the lazy adolescent becomes active, the girl who wouldn't leave her bed moves instantly at her child's cry. Love turns the miser into a benefactor, the impatient into someone with endurance — even the selfish bird that once hoarded grain becomes the one calling its chicks to share it. And beyond behavior, love brings *inspiration* — 'the nightingale learnt its song by the favour of the rose'; Majnun's madness came not from within himself but from Layla's glance.

But Mutahhari adds an important caution: love's effects on the *body* and on the *spirit* are opposite. In the body, it can be destructive — pallor, sleeplessness, disorder in the digestive and nervous systems. But in the spirit, it's purifying — it removes envy, avarice, cowardice, laziness, and self-admiration; it takes bitterness and makes it sweet, copper and makes it gold."

**Key Questions to Remember:**
- Where does the term "elixir" for love come from, historically, and what did the alchemists originally mean by it?
- Describe the hen example — what does it illustrate about love's effect on courage?
- According to the text, what is the *difference* between love's effect on the body versus its effect on the spirit?
- Name at least three negative qualities the text says love purifies from the spirit.

## 5. Breaking Down the Barriers (Ch. 12)

**What to say:**
"Mutahhari's final move here is to explain *why* love has all these effects — and his answer is that love pulls a person out of self-love and self-enclosure. He describes self-love as a defensive barrier: as long as we're locked inside it, we're weak — timid, avaricious, quick-tempered, cut off from real vitality. Love breaks that barrier down.

But he's careful to clarify something important: this doesn't mean we're supposed to eliminate legitimate self-regard, or stop liking ourselves. That's not the goal, and it's not even coherent. What he means is that the *self* itself needs to expand — the boundary that treats everything outside 'me' as foreign needs to be pushed outward until it takes in other human beings, and ideally the whole of creation. So the struggle with self-love isn't a struggle to erase the self — it's a struggle against its *limitations*. Love is what redirects our drives and affections outward, enlarging our existence and shifting the very center of our being — which is why Mutahhari calls it one of the greatest moral and educative forces there is, as long as it's guided correctly."

**Key Questions to Remember:**
- What does Mutahhari mean by "self-love" as a "defensive barrier"?
- Does Mutahhari say the goal is to *eliminate* self-regard? What does he say the real goal is instead?
- In his own words, what is the difference between "reducing" a person and "perfecting" a person?

## 6. Closing

**What to say:**
"So we've come full circle. We started with a hadith telling us that love and hate, rightly directed, are the strongest handhold of faith. And everything we've covered today about 'Ali — his attraction reaching across centuries, love that survives punishment because it's rooted in truth, and love's power to transform weakness into strength — is one man embodying that single principle completely. He is, in Mutahhari's own words, like the laws of nature which remain unchanged by time — a well-spring which is never dry, but which increases day by day."

**Key Questions to Remember (Full Session Review):**
- What makes 'Ali's power of attraction "three-dimensional" rather than one- or two-dimensional?
- How does the story of the punished man (fingers cut off) prove that love for 'Ali isn't self-interested?
- What is the difference between love's effect on the body and its effect on the spirit?
- What does it mean to "expand the self" rather than eliminate self-love?
- Why does Mutahhari say 'Ali "became more alive" after his martyrdom rather than being forgotten?
