# Book 03 — Teaching Guide
## Jihad bil-Nafs · Ayatollah Mazaheri

This guide is an instructional layer over the canonical learning data. It is deliberately **not** a replacement for the source. Teaching should move repeatedly between the original chapter, the learning chunks, the conceptual connections, and the question bank.

## Teaching philosophy

The central pedagogical mistake with a book like this is to turn it into a vocabulary list of virtues: patience, repentance, sincerity, prayer, remembrance, trust. The stronger approach is to ask how these practices form one another.

Use this sequence for each lesson:

**source → explain → challenge → ordinary-life question → retrieval → application → source return**

The general-public questions are especially useful here. They surface the objections and confusions that a knowledgeable reader may silently have.

## Five-part course architecture

### Part I — Formation of the person (Units 01–05)
**Core arc:** education → natural drives → human spiritual capacity → struggle → commanding self.

The instructor should establish early that *jihad al-nafs* is not presented as hatred of the human person or destruction of natural desire. Unit 02's distinction between desire and the way desire is satisfied is essential preparation for Unit 03 and Unit 05.

**Teaching questions**
- What is education trying to form, not merely what information is being transmitted?
- If desires are natural, why do they need discipline?
- What does “struggle with the self” look like when nothing dramatic is happening?
- Why can knowing the right thing fail to produce the right action?

**Activity:** Give learners three ordinary situations involving money, status, anger, or appetite. Ask them to distinguish (a) the natural desire, (b) the chosen means, (c) the moral direction, and (d) the habit being strengthened.

**Assessment:** Compare Units 02, 03, and 05. Explain what each adds that the previous unit did not.

### Part II — Patience becomes character (Units 06–08)
**Core arc:** performing patience → acquiring patience as a stable disposition → seeing its effects.

Do not teach *sabr* as passive suffering. Ask learners to distinguish endurance from resignation, and patience from avoiding necessary action.

**Teaching questions**
- What is the difference between surviving difficulty and responding well to it?
- How does repeated behavior become a stable quality of character?
- What changes when patience becomes a disposition rather than an occasional effort?

**Activity:** Learners describe one recurring situation in which they lose patience. Rewrite the situation as a sequence of trigger → impulse → choice → repeated response → emerging habit.

**Assessment:** Explain why Unit 07 cannot simply be collapsed into Unit 06.

### Part III — Repair, inner breadth, and speech (Units 09–14)
**Core arc:** repentance → delayed repentance → repair → inner spaciousness → speech discipline.

This section should be taught with care. Repentance is not merely emotional guilt, and patience is not permission to tolerate injustice. Unit 11 is especially useful for introducing the idea that moral repair can involve other people.

**Teaching questions**
- Is regret enough?
- Why is “I will change later” itself a problem?
- What obligations remain when another person has been harmed?
- Does telling the truth always justify saying something?
- What does inner spaciousness look like under insult or pressure?

**Activity:** Present a fictional conflict involving anger, gossip, or public embarrassment. Have learners identify what should be stopped, what should be repaired, what should be said, and what should remain unsaid.

**Assessment:** Construct a response to a wrongdoing that includes regret, decision, repair, and prevention of repetition.

### Part IV — Devotion becomes a method (Units 15–22)
**Core arc:** servitude → disciplined activity → sincerity → night prayer → Qur'an → supplication → Ahl al-Bayt.

This section should not become a catalogue of recommended practices. The teaching question is **why these practices are supposed to transform the person**.

The major conceptual hub in the canonical graph is Unit 17, *ikhlas*. Use it as a bridge: an outwardly good action can still be spiritually distorted by the motive that controls it.

**Teaching questions**
- What does servitude change about ordinary work?
- Why is activity and perseverance discussed alongside repentance?
- How can a person detect the desire for praise inside a good action?
- Why would repeated prayer, Qur'an, or supplication matter rather than one intense experience?
- What role does recourse to Ahl al-Bayt play in the book's model of formation?

**Activity:** Take one outwardly good action and generate three possible motives: sincere service, reputation seeking, and mixed motivation. Ask what evidence could distinguish them and what correction would look like.

**Assessment:** Explain how Units 15–17 form a sequence rather than three unrelated virtues.

### Part V — Orientation: remembrance, reliance, tawhid (Units 23–25)
**Core arc:** remembering God → trusting God → understanding reliance through tawhid.

The final units should be taught as a culmination, not an unrelated theological appendix. Unit 24's distinction between human responsibility and divine outcome is particularly important: *tawakkul* does not mean refusing to act.

**Teaching questions**
- If someone already believes in God, why does remembrance need repetition?
- Does trust in God remove the need for planning?
- What is the difference between using a cause and treating the cause as ultimate?
- How does tawhid change the emotional meaning of success and failure?

**Activity:** Give learners a situation where a person has done everything reasonably possible but cannot control the outcome. Ask them to separate: responsibility, effort, uncertainty, outcome, and trust.

**Assessment:** Trace how the final teaching on tawhid and tawakkul reinterprets at least three earlier practices.

## Cross-unit seminar prompts

1. **Desire and discipline:** Compare Units 02, 03, and 05. Is the enemy desire itself, or the person's relationship to desire?
2. **Habit and character:** Compare Units 06 and 07. When does an action become a trait?
3. **Repentance and time:** Compare Units 09–11. Why does delay become morally significant?
4. **Speech and self-control:** Connect Unit 14 to the earlier treatment of the nafs.
5. **Means and sincerity:** Connect Units 16–17. Can perseverance itself become ego-driven?
6. **Devotion and formation:** Connect Units 18–22. What is the function of repeated devotional practice?
7. **Remembrance and trust:** Connect Units 23–24. Why might remembrance precede reliance?
8. **Tawhid as culmination:** Connect Unit 25 backward through the entire book. Does theology here function as lived psychology and ethics, or only doctrine?

## Assessment design

Use the 75-question bank in three passes:

- **Pass A — Retrieval:** 25 reader questions. The learner should answer without notes.
- **Pass B — Translation:** 25 general-public questions. The learner must explain the teaching in ordinary language and give an example.
- **Pass C — Synthesis:** 25 advanced questions. The learner must connect units and distinguish similar-looking ideas.

A learner should not be considered ready after memorizing definitions. A stronger threshold is:

1. Can state the principle.
2. Can locate its source unit.
3. Can explain it without specialist vocabulary.
4. Can answer a plausible objection.
5. Can connect it to another unit.
6. Can apply it without changing the principle into a slogan.

## Instructor caution

The book contains theological, ethical, and spiritual claims. Do not silently present every interpretive explanation in this guide as a direct quotation from Mazaheri. Mark the difference between **what the source says**, **what the learning layer infers**, and **what an instructor proposes as an application**.
