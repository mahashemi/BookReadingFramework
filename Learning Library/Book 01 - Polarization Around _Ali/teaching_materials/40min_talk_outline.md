# The Power of Attraction in 'Ali
### 40-Minute Talk Outline (with Audience Interaction) — This Session's Material
Source: *Polarization Around the Character of Imam 'Ali*, Ayatullah Murtadha Mutahhari — Part 2: "The Power of Attraction in 'Ali" (Powerful Attractions → Breaking Down the Barriers). Previous session's material (the Introduction) is summarized only as a 5-min recap below and kept in full in the Appendix for your own reference.

**How to use the 🎤 prompts:** each one is a quick show-of-hands, a 30-second turn-to-your-neighbor, or a direct "someone shout out an answer" — not a long discussion. Keep them fast so they punctuate the talk rather than derail your timing.

---

## 0. Refresher — Previous Session (5 min)
Last time we covered the Introduction: the universal law of attraction, and 'Ali as "one man with two powers." Keep this fast and punchy — it's a recap, not a re-teach.

- **The universal law:** Attraction and repulsion isn't just human emotion — modern science says every atom in existence, from the smallest particle to the largest mass, is under a general law of attraction. Ancient people only recognized *particular* cases (magnet–iron, amber–straw); they didn't grasp the universal law.
- **Attraction/repulsion among humans:** Not about romantic attraction — about ordinary friendship, affection, enmity. Root cause: either *resemblance* (the Rumi story — a raven and stork who turn out to both be lame in one leg) or *contrariety* (real opposition).
- **Four kinds of people:** (1) attract nothing, repel nothing — "a slab of rock," hollow and vacuous; (2) attract everyone, repel no one — usually hypocrisy, since real conviction always creates some friction (illustrated by 'Ali and Ibn Muljam: *"I want him to live; he wants to kill me"*); (3) repel everyone, attract no one — also deficient; (4) attract **and** repel — people who live by real conviction. Having both powers strongly isn't proof of virtue on its own — even Genghis Khan, Hajjaj, and Muʿawiyah had both.
- **'Ali, one man with two powers:** His attraction and repulsion were unmatched in any century — friends who'd give their lives for him, memory that still moves even enemies to tears centuries later. His own words: *"After my place has been vacated and someone else has occupied it, you will know me."*

**🎤 Interaction:** "Before we move to today's material — someone remind the room: what were the four types of people we covered last time, and which one did 'Ali belong to?"

**Bridge line into today:** "Last time we established *that* 'Ali had this extraordinary two-sided power. Today we go deeper — we're going to look at exactly *how* that attraction actually worked, how far it reached across history, and what love itself does to a human being when it's real."

## 1. Opening — Hadiths from Al-Kāfi (3 min)
Open with the Al-Kāfi chapter "Love for the Sake of Allah and Hate for the Sake of Allah" (Vol. 2, Bk 1, Ch. 60) to frame love/hate as a principle of faith, not just emotion:

- Imam al-Sadiq (a.s.): asked whether love and hate are part of belief, he replied *"Is belief anything but love and hate?"* — then recited Qur'an 49:7 on faith being made beloved and disbelief made hateful to the heart.
- The Prophet (s): told his companions the strongest handhold of belief is not prayer, fasting, or hajj alone, but **love for the sake of Allah, hate for the sake of Allah**, loyalty to His friends and disavowal of His enemies.
- Imam al-Baqir (a.s.): whoever does not love and hate on the basis of religion has, in effect, no religion.

**Transition line:** "Today's talk is about exactly this — how attraction reached all the way through history and into hearts, how love and justice worked together in 'Ali, and what real love actually does to a person."

**🎤 Interaction:** "By a show of hands, who here has ever heard the phrase 'love for the sake of Allah, hate for the sake of Allah' before tonight?"

## 2. Powerful Attractions Across Centuries (Ch. 9) — 7 min
Frame with Mutahhari's three-level model of "calls" in human history:
- **One-dimensional** calls: spread widely, then fade.
- **Two-dimensional** calls: spread widely *and* last through time.
- **Three-dimensional** calls: spread widely, last through centuries, *and* penetrate to the depth of the human heart. This, he says, is unique to the chain of the prophets — and 'Ali's power of attraction belongs to this last category.

Tell both historical illustrations:
- **Maytham at-Tammar**: twenty years after 'Ali's martyrdom, under Umayyad repression when all freedoms were crushed, he spoke from his own crucifixion about 'Ali's virtues, drawing crowds — until the authorities gagged him and eventually killed him.
- **Ibn as-Sikkit**: a great scholar of Arabic literature, tutor to Caliph al-Mutawakkil's children roughly 200 years after 'Ali's death. When al-Mutawakkil provocatively asked whether his own sons were dearer to Ibn as-Sikkit than Hasan and Husayn, Ibn as-Sikkit answered that even 'Ali's slave Qanbar was dearer to him than the caliph's sons and the caliph himself — and had his tongue cut out for it.

Also worth including from this section:
- 'Ali's own words: *"Those who amass wealth are dead even when they are alive, but those with knowledge will remain as long as the world remains."* And on his own legacy: *"After my place has been vacated and someone else has occupied it, you will know me."*
- Mutahhari's line that if 'Ali had had no divine colouring, "he would have been forgotten," like every other champion of speech, knowledge, or the battlefield — but instead "he became more alive" after death.
- The closing image: 'Ali is "like the laws of nature which remain unchanged by time... a well-spring of munificence which is never dry."

**Point to land:** This is love strong enough to override self-preservation, centuries after the person being loved has died.

**🎤 Interaction:** "Take a guess: how many years after 'Ali's death do you think someone was still willing to be executed just for praising him publicly? Shout out a number." *(Reveal: twenty years — Maytham at-Tammar.)*

**🎤 Interaction:** "Ask the person next to you: what do you think it actually cost Ibn as-Sikkit, in that moment, to answer honestly instead of flattering the caliph? Ten seconds, then I'll take one answer." *(Payoff: he lost his tongue for it.)*

## 3. Shi'ism, the Religion of Love (Ch. 10) — 6 min
- Root this in the Prophet's words: *"'Ali and his party (Shi'ah) will be the triumphant ones."*
- 'Ali as the "standard and criterion" for testing human natures: a sound nature will never truly turn against 'Ali even under his sword; a diseased nature won't love him even after receiving favors from him.
- 'Ali's own words on why this is so: even if he struck a true believer with his sword, it wouldn't make that believer his enemy; and if he poured the whole world's wealth on a hypocrite's head, it would never make the hypocrite love him — because "the believer will never be your enemy and the hypocrite will never love you," as the Prophet decreed.
- Tell the story of the man whose hand 'Ali cut off for wrongdoing — when the Kharijite Ibn al-Kawwa' mockingly asks who cut off his hand, the man responds by extolling 'Ali at length, ending: *"Why should I not extol him, now that his friendship is mixed with flesh and blood? ... he did not cut off my hand except with a right that God has established."* This is love for the sake of truth and justice, not personal benefit.

**Point to land:** Real love for 'Ali survives punishment, because it's rooted in recognizing truth — not in personal comfort or favor.

**🎤 Interaction:** "Before I tell you what the man said — guess: after having his hand cut off, do you think he cursed 'Ali, stayed silent, or praised him? Show of hands for each." *(Reveal: he launched into an extended, genuine tribute.)*

## 4. The Elixir of Love (Ch. 11) — 7 min
- Persian poets called love "the elixir" — the alchemists' fabled substance (also called "the philosopher's stone") that transmutes one matter into another. Poets said the real transmuting elixir is love itself — "people are mines, like gold-mines and silver-mines," and love is what refines them.
- Effects of love, drawn directly from the text — pick 3–4 to develop live:
  - **Courage from fear**: the hen that flees from a child alone, but becomes fierce once she has chicks to defend — wings lowered in a posture of defense, clucking louder, attacking instead of fleeing.
  - **Diligence from laziness**: the once-lazy adolescent boy becoming active, the once-sleepy girl moving "like lightning" at her child's cry.
  - **Generosity from selfishness**: the bird that once hoarded grain for itself now calls its chicks to share it; the miser becomes a benefactor.
  - **Endurance from impatience**: the once-spoiled young woman becoming patient and persevering through hunger and sleeplessness as a mother.
  - **Inspiration**: "The nightingale learnt its song by the favour of the rose" — and Majnun's madness came not from within himself, but from "the glance of Layla."
- Note the caution: in the body, love's effects can be *destructive* — pallor, sleeplessness, disorder in the digestive and nervous systems — but in the spirit, it is described as purifying: it removes envy, avarice, cowardice, laziness, conceitedness, self-admiration, grudges and malevolence. "By love, bitternesses became sweet, / By love, pieces of copper became gold."

**Point to land:** Love isn't just a feeling — Mutahhari treats it as an active force that transforms weakness into strength, selfishness into generosity, and dullness into inspiration.

**🎤 Interaction:** "The text gives us the hen who becomes brave once she has chicks. Can someone in the room share — even briefly — a time love (for a person, a cause, a craft) made *you* braver or more disciplined than you normally are?" *(Take 1–2 volunteers max, keep it moving.)*

## 5. Breaking Down the Barriers (Ch. 12) — 5 min
- Love pulls a person *out* of self-love and self-enclosure, regardless of the kind of love — animal, parental, or human.
- Self-love is described as a "defensive barrier" — man is weak, timid, avaricious, covetous, misanthropic, quick-tempered, selfish and arrogant *until* he steps outside his own self.
- Important clarification: the goal isn't to eliminate legitimate self-regard ("amour-propre") — that's not even coherent. The real goal is to *expand* the self outward: "The personality must expand to take in every other human being, if not the whole of the universe of creation."
- The reform of man "does not lie in reducing him, it lies in perfecting and adding to him" — the struggle with self-love is a struggle with the *limitations* of the self, not an attempt to erase the self.
- Quote: *"Whoever's garment is torn by love / Is entirely cleansed of covetousness or blemish."*

**Point to land:** Love is what redirects a person's drives and affections outward — "it enlarges his existence and changes the focal point of his being" — which is why Mutahhari calls it one of the greatest moral and educative forces there is.

**🎤 Interaction:** "Last one before we close — in your own words, in one sentence: what's the difference between 'expanding the self through love' and just being a people-pleaser (the Type 2 we recapped earlier)?" *(Answer to draw toward: real love follows a path and coincides with reality, so it still creates enemies; people-pleasing avoids that entirely.)*

---

## 6. Closing (2 min)
Bring it back to the opening hadiths: Islam does not ask believers to only love, or only reject — it asks for both, aligned with truth. Everything covered today — 'Ali's centuries-spanning attraction, love that survives punishment, and love's power to transform weakness into strength — is Mutahhari's case for what it means to have "two powers," properly guided.

**Suggested closing line, adapted from the text's own words on 'Ali:**
*"He is like the laws of nature which remain unchanged by time — a well-spring which is never dry, but which rather increases day by day."*

## Appendix — Full Previous-Session Detail (Reference Only, Not for This Session)
*Kept here for your own prep — do not re-teach this in full today, only use the Section 0 recap above.*

### The Universal Law of Attraction (Ch. 4)
- Modern science holds that no atom in existence escapes the law of general attraction — from the largest masses to the smallest atoms.
- Ancient man only recognized *particular* instances (magnet–iron, amber–straw), not the *universal* law.
- Early debates about why the earth stays fixed in space: some said it's pulled equally from all sides; others said it's *repelled* equally from all sides.
- Even biology was explained this way: the ancients believed the body has attractive, repulsive, digestive and retentive faculties — the stomach "draws in" food, the liver "draws in" water.

### Attraction and Repulsion Among Human Beings (Ch. 5)
- Not about sexual attraction — about the friendships, affections, enmities and hatreds of social life.
- Thesis: rooted in **general resemblance and similarity**, or **opposition and mutual aversion** (tadadd).
- The Rumi raven-and-stork story: a wise man discovers the secret bond between two unlike birds — both are lame in one leg.
- Competing theory: attraction/repulsion rooted in *need* — man is a creature in want, drawn to whoever fills his emptiness.

### Four Kinds of People (Ch. 6)
**Type 1 — Attract nothing, repel nothing:** like "a slab of rock," hollow and vacuous, because man needs to love and be loved, and also needs to hate and be hated.

**Type 2 — Attract everyone, repel no one:** the Zamzam/cremation poem; usually hypocrisy, since real conviction creates friction. Christian/Hindu "love only" philosophy critiqued: love must "coincide with reality," and reality has a path. 'Ali and Ibn Muljam: *"I want him to live; he wants to kill me."*

**Type 3 — Repel everyone, attract no one:** also deficient — there is always *some* good in humanity that should draw someone. 'Ali: *"The most powerless person is he who is unable to find any friends..."*

**Type 4 — Attract and repel, both:** people who live by real conviction. Strength varies; can outlast the person by centuries. Not proof of virtue alone — Genghis Khan, Hajjaj, Muʿawiyah had both powers. What matters is *who* is attracted and *who* is repelled. Nadir Shah example. Closing image: like Newton's gravity, pull is proportional to "mass" and inversely to distance.

### 'Ali — One Man with Two Powers (Ch. 7)
- 'Ali's attraction and repulsion unmatched in any century; friends willing to be sacrificed for him; even enemies moved to tears centuries later.
- The bond is not material — a different order of connection.
- *"Tomorrow you will see these days of mine ... after my place has been vacated and someone else has occupied it, you will know me."*
