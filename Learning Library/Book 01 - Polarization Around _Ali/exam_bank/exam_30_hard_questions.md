## Section B — Model Answers

**21.** (1) Attracts nothing, repels nothing — "a slab of rock" among men, an animal who eats, sleeps and walks; (2) attracts all, repels none; (3) repels all, attracts none; (4) both attracts and repels. Type 1 is most damning because Mutahhari defines "positive" as including *wickedness* as well as virtue — such a person has no positive quality in either direction. Man is created needing to love and be loved, and also to hate and be hated; Type 1 satisfies neither. He compares such a person to a sheep, cared for only because its meat will eventually be consumed. They start "no wave of approval, nor any wave of disapproval" — they are hollow and vacuous.

**22.** One-dimensional calls spread broadly then vanish into oblivion. Two-dimensional calls spread broadly *and* extend into the temporal dimension. Three-dimensional calls do both and also penetrate the depths of the human spirit, ruling "in the profundity of the soul" — exclusive to the chain of the prophets. Mutahhari places 'Ali's attraction in this third category. He states explicitly that had 'Ali lacked divine colouring and not been a man of God, he would have been forgotten like every other champion of speech, knowledge, power, or the battlefield — but instead "he became more alive" after being killed.

**23.** A believing friend of 'Ali fell into error and had the fingers of his right hand amputated under Divine Law. Ibn al-Kawwa', a seditious Kharijite, approached with feigned compassion to exploit the moment politically. Instead of resentment the man delivered an extended eulogy — "chief of the Prophet's successors… Imam of right guidance… the champion of Mecca" — concluding that 'Ali's friendship is "mixed with flesh and blood" and that the amputation was by a right God established. This illustrates 'Ali's own saying (Nahj al-Balaghah, Saying 42): striking a believer with his sword cannot create enmity; pouring the world's wealth on a hypocrite cannot create love — because the Prophet decreed the believer will never be his enemy and the hypocrite will never love him. 'Ali is thus the criterion (*mi'yar*) for assaying human nature.

**24.** The first kind originates in the passions, especially the sexual, is tied to the physiological activity of the genital organs and to youth, comes quickly, goes quickly, and "kills virtue"; union is its graveyard. The second is *noble sentiment* — Qur'anic *muwaddah wa rahmah* (30:21) — in which the beloved is honoured, the lover seeks the beloved's happiness and will sacrifice for it; this produces purity, tenderness, altruism, and *grows stronger* with union. Mutahhari's careful position: passionate love *can* become beneficial, but only when joined to chastity and piety, under conditions of separation and inaccessibility, where the spirit bears the pressure without succumbing — hence the tradition that one who loves, conceals it, remains chaste and dies is a martyr. But he insists it must never be *recommended*: like affliction, it may perfect a soul that endures it, yet no one may manufacture an affliction for himself or others. He adds that love, more than anything, is against reason and ousts it from governance.

**25.** On the final day of Siffin, with defeat one step away, Mu'awiyah — advised by 'Amr ibn al-'As — had Qur'ans raised on spear-points calling for the Book to arbitrate. 'Ali identified it immediately as a ruse: they were using the paper and binding of the Qur'an to destroy its meaning, and "it is I who am the reality and the true manifestation of the Qur'an." He invoked the *jihad* ruling on enemies shielding themselves behind Muslim captives. A large body of undiscriminating pietists refused, threatening to attack 'Ali from behind and to hack him to pieces, forcing him to recall Malik al-Ashtar — who was moments from taking Mu'awiyah's command pavilion. They then forced the selection of Abu Musa al-Ash'ari over 'Ali's proposals of Ibn 'Abbas or Malik al-Ashtar. 'Amr tricked Abu Musa into deposing 'Ali first, then installed Mu'awiyah. Seeing the scandal, the same group realised their error but misdiagnosed it — concluding not that they had blundered in halting the war or choosing Abu Musa, but that appointing *any* human arbitrator was unbelief, since judgement belongs to God alone. From this misdiagnosis the sect was born.

**26.** (a) They declared 'Ali, 'Uthman, Mu'awiyah, the fighters at Jamal, and all who accepted arbitration to be infidels — excepting those who voted for arbitration and then repented. (b) They declared infidels anyone who did *not* accept (a). (c) Faith was not sincere belief alone but belief compounded with action — so that major sin expelled one from Islam. (d) Revolt against an unjust ruler was unconditionally obligatory, and "bidding to good and forbidding evil" was subject to no conditions whatever. Consequence: holding all four, they began from the position that every human being on earth was an infidel whose blood was of no value and who was condemned to the Fire — which is why their movement issued directly in indiscriminate violence.

## Section C — Model Answers

**27.** Speaker: 'Ali, during the Battle of Jamal, answering a companion paralysed by doubt. The dilemma: on one side 'Ali and figures like 'Ammar; on the other 'A'ishah (of whom the Qur'an says the Prophet's wives are as mothers to the believers), Talhah (an early forerunner with a distinguished record) and az-Zubayr (with an even better record, who had stood in 'Ali's house on the day of Saqifah). The man could not believe such personalities could be on the side of falsehood. Mutahhari is emphatic that the man should not be judged too harshly — we judge easily only because history has since clarified matters, or because we were taught the answer in infancy. 'Ali's reply inverts the epistemology: one must be a knower of truth and falsehood, not a knower of personalities; truth is the standard by which individuals are measured, not vice versa. Mutahhari identifies this precisely as the *spirit* of Shi'ism — partisanship for the principles of Islam rather than the personalities of Islam — which he contrasts with merely holding the Shi'ah *motto* ("'Ali is the caliph after the Prophet"). He notes Salman, Abu Dharr, al-Miqdad and 'Ammar gathered round 'Ali on exactly this basis. Taha Husayn, the Egyptian scholar, said that after revelation ceased and the call from heaven ended, no words of such greatness were heard.

**28.** Speaker: the Prophet Muhammad (s), cited by Mutahhari in "The Politics of 'Using' the Qur'an." Significance: Mutahhari argues the most dangerous affliction of the Muslim community is not material poverty but *poverty of thought*. Whenever the ignorant put on a display of sanctity and the people mistake them for the model Muslim, an instrument is handed to unscrupulous schemers — "setting the power of Islam itself to work against Islam." He explicitly names Western colonialism as having profited from this technique, especially in manufacturing schisms. This links directly to his treatment of hypocrisy (*nifaq*) as harder to fight than open unbelief, because unbelief is unconcealed whereas hypocrisy wears an outward face of Islam over an inward face of unbelief — and to 'Ali's letter to Muhammad ibn Abi Bakr, where the Prophet says he fears neither the believer nor the unbeliever for his community, but the one hypocritical in heart and learned in speech. Hence Mutahhari's conclusion: fighting stupidity *is* fighting hypocrisy, because the stupid are the swords in the hypocrite's hand.

## Section D — Marking Guidance

**29.** Strong answers will reconstruct: love must "coincide with reality"; reality entails a path; a path creates enemies. They should handle the Prophet as *rahmatan li'l-'alamin* (21:107) without collapsing it into universal approval — Mutahhari distinguishes loving an enemy from approving his inclinations, calling the latter hypocrisy. The Ibn Muljam case should be used precisely: 'Ali loved him, refused pre-emptive punishment ("Should I punish before the crime?"), yet never abandoned justice. Credit *critical* engagement: Mutahhari's characterisation of "some Christians" and "some Hindus" is brief and does not engage their theologians directly; he also concedes Gandhi's "This is my religion" as *supporting* his own point, which complicates the critique. Note his footnote that 'Ali named his horses, swords and turbans, and said of Uhud "it is a mountain which loves us and we love it" — evidence Mutahhari offers that 'Ali was the paradigm of love, not its opponent.

**30.** Should reconstruct the Socratic/philosophical method: reform by intellect, reasoning and reckoning — likened to plucking nose-hairs one by one, a farmer pulling tares by hand, or cleaning wheat of stones by hand. Against this, the way of love: find a perfect being and bind one's heart to him. The images: acid-etching versus scratching with a nail; separating iron filings by hand versus one sweep of a magnet. Supporting evidence: love causes resemblance (Tusi on *Isharat*); followers imitate a beloved's gait and dress involuntarily; Rumi transformed by Shams-e Tabrizi "like a fuse falling into a gunpowder store." Also the qualifier: "exertion and being drawn… must go together" — neither effort without attraction nor attraction without effort suffices. **Strongest weakness candidates:** (i) Mutahhari himself concedes love brings blindness and deafness (*hubb al-shay' yu'mi wa yusimm*), makes the lover heedless of the beloved's defects, and makes attention one-sided — so the instrument distorts perception even as it purifies; (ii) everything depends on correctly choosing the beloved, but the faculty needed for that choice is precisely the discriminating intellect the method subordinates; (iii) he says love's "equilibrium is not a function of the scales of goodness" but of the lover's own latent capacity — which risks making the transformation independent of the beloved's actual merit.

**31.** Should distinguish motto (slogan, nominal affiliation) from spirit (underlying disposition), and show both directions: one may hold a sect's motto while lacking its spirit, or possess its spirit while rejecting its slogans. Applied to Shi'ism: the motto is "'Ali is caliph after the Prophet"; the spirit is criticism, research, exactitude, partisanship for principles over personalities — so a man may be Shi'ah by imitation and speech yet "Kharijite in spirit and practice." Applied to Khawarijism: the sect is extinct, but its spirit survives as rigidity, literalism, takfir, narrow-mindedness, and above all the *divorce of intellection from religiosity* — which Mutahhari traces into the Ash'arites, the exoterists/traditionists, and the Akhbari school, and even into contemporary "intellectuals" dressing it in empirical philosophy. On 'Ali's sword: the Khawarij had calloused foreheads, hands hardened like camels' feet, tattered shirts, nights spent in prayer. 'Ali's own claim — "I have put out the eye of revolt; no one had the daring to do this except me" — rests on the fact that only someone of his insight and faith could strike men of such visible piety without wavering; an ordinary believer would have been paralysed, and only an unbeliever could have done it for the wrong reasons. Note his two images: *ghayhab* (gloom, producing doubt) and *kalab* (rabidity — the rabid dog that infects all it bites). Also that 'Ali's action licensed later rulers to fight them.

**32.** Should establish the democratic phase concretely: he did not imprison, flog, or cut their stipends from the *baytu'l-mal*; they were free everywhere to state their views and were answered with argument; they disrupted his sermons and he released the man who insulted him ("It was only me he insulted"); Ibn al-Kawwa' repeatedly shouted Qur'an 39:65 at him mid-prayer, and 'Ali fell silent each time in obedience to 7:204, finally reciting 30:60 and continuing. Mutahhari calls this degree of freedom possibly without precedent. **What changed:** they moved from expressing beliefs to armed insurrection — threatening the security of the highways, marauding, sedition, and an armed uprising aimed at bringing down the legitimate government. 'Ali then pursued them to Nahrawan, still first offering argument and the flag of Abu Ayyub, so that 8,000 of 12,000 withdrew. **On whether the line is principled:** credit both directions. For: it tracks the familiar and defensible distinction between protected speech and violent action, and 'Ali exhausted persuasion first. Against: strong answers may press whether Mutahhari's own framing strains it — he describes 'Ali as fighting a *way of thinking* rather than individuals ("if these individuals had not thought in this way, 'Ali would not have… spilt their blood so that these ideas would die with them"), which sits awkwardly with a purely conduct-based justification. Reward candidates who notice this tension rather than smoothing it over.
